# The Twenty-Four Microseasons of Albany Hill
### A phenological calendar for the eastern Helderberg upland — Westerlo, New York (~1,200 ft, east-facing)

---

## How to Read This Calendar

This calendar divides the year into twenty-four microseasons of roughly fifteen days each, in the spirit of the East Asian solar-term tradition — but every season is named for what this particular hill actually does. Setting: ~1,200 feet on an east-facing slope of the Helderberg upland, with Still Hill (~1,800 ft) standing to the west, stealing the last two hours of afternoon sun and blunting the prevailing westerlies. Limestone bones below, acidic skin above — decades of leaf litter, conifer duff, and leaching have soured the topsoil despite the sweet bedrock. *(Both premises are since corrected: the bedrock here is more likely Hamilton Group shale than Helderberg limestone, and the soils are mapped as naturally acidic — see the companion document.)*

**The water goes south and west, not east.** The stream along the property's eastern edge is the upper **Eightmile Creek**, which rises about two and a half miles north-northeast at **Onderdonk Lake** — known in the nineteenth century as **Mud Hollow Pond**, dammed as a millpond to serve the South Berne Mill a mile north, and described then as holding four- to ten-foot stumps of drowned trees across much of its upper end, with no habitation on its shores. Eightmile Creek runs south out of Westerlo into Rensselaerville and joins **Tenmile Creek at the hamlet of Medusa**; Tenmile, which rises at **Lake Myosotis** in Rensselaerville two miles due west of this hill, carries on to **Catskill Creek** and thence to the **Hudson**.

**Basic Creek is the next basin east, not this one.** Basic Creek runs roughly three miles east and drains by an entirely separate route — into Hannacrois Creek and to the Hudson at Coeymans — with the Basic Creek Reservoir, built 1928–32 by flooding former farmland, feeding Albany's municipal water supply. This hill sits close to the divide between the two systems, and its own water leaves by the Catskill Creek side.

Practical consequences, baked into every date below: this land runs **3–4°F colder than Albany**. Spring arrives **7–10 days later** than the Hudson Valley; the first fall frost lands **7–10 days earlier**. Last spring frost ~May 20–25; first fall frost typically mid-to-late September; effective growing season roughly **110–120 frost-free days**. When a field guide says "mid-April," read early May. All dates here are already adjusted — hill dates, not valley dates.

Astronomical events included are the *fixed* kind — solstices, equinoxes, annual meteor showers, the seasonal wheel of constellations — so this calendar never expires. Haudenosaunee seasonal knowledge appears where it genuinely belongs; the hill sits within the ancestral homelands of the Mohawk (Kanien'kehá:ka), the People of the Flint, eastern keepers of the Haudenosaunee longhouse door.

**Ghost bullets** appear throughout, marked *Ghost:* — the absences that shape this landscape as surely as anything still standing. A calendar of what is, laid over a calendar of what was.

**Health watch bullets**, marked *Health watch:*, flag what is diagnosable in each specific window. Tree problems are visible on a schedule: hemlock adelgid in winter, beech leaf disease only in backlit June, premature fall color in late July, ash blonding in September. Miss the fortnight and the symptom is invisible for another year. A companion document, *Reading Decline*, carries the full diagnostic detail.

## The Deeper Calendar: Layers Under the Year

Four timescales are legible from this hill simultaneously. Any one walk crosses all of them.

**~420–400 million years ago — the shallow sea.** The Helderberg limestone was laid down grain by grain on the floor of a warm, shallow tropical sea, when this rock sat roughly twenty-five degrees *south* of the equator, on the drifting margin of a continent that no longer exists. The Helderberg Group formations — Manlius, Coeymans, Kalkberg, New Scotland, Becraft — are a world-famous stratigraphic sequence, and among the most fossiliferous rocks in eastern North America. Brachiopods, crinoid stems, solitary and colonial corals, bryozoans, the occasional trilobite. The escarpment's chert nodules — the flint of the People of the Flint — are the silica of sponge spicules and radiolarians, dissolved and reprecipitated over hundreds of millions of years into stone that fractures conchoidally and takes an edge sharper than surgical steel. Roughly thirty miles southwest, at Gilboa, stand the fossil stumps of the oldest known forest on Earth, ~385 million years old, from the era when trees were inventing themselves.

**~50,000 years ago — the long cold.** Mid-Wisconsinan. Ice was gathering to the north and the climate here was periglacial — tundra and cold steppe, or ice outright, in a see-saw of advances. The honest answer is that almost nothing is known in detail, because the last ice erased its own earlier drafts. What is certain is that any evidence of this hill's condition then was ground to powder by what came next.

**~26,000–14,700 years ago — the ice and its lake.** The Laurentide Ice Sheet buried this entire landscape under something on the order of a mile of ice, its weight depressing the crust hundreds of feet. When it withdrew, the Hudson Valley filled with **Glacial Lake Albany**, a long proglacial lake whose highest shorelines sat near 300–330 feet in elevation. From this hilltop, one would have looked *down* on open water — a cold lake filling the valley to the east while the Helderberg upland stood as headland above it. The sand plains of the Albany Pine Bush are that lake's delta, sorted and abandoned. Every erratic boulder in the woods, every polished-and-scratched limestone surface on the escarpment, dates to this withdrawal. The Wisconsin ice receded from the Helderberg Plateau itself approximately 14,700 years ago — roughly three thousand years before the first firm evidence of people in the region.

**How long the ice took to leave.** Not a melt so much as a slow withdrawal punctuated by one abrupt collapse. The ice stood at or near its maximum southern extent — the terminal moraine that is now Long Island, Nantucket, and the ridges of northern New Jersey — from roughly **26,000 to 21,000 years ago**. Initial retreat began around 22,000 years ago and was almost imperceptibly slow, on the order of **5 to 25 meters a year**, driven by rising summer insolation rather than any dramatic warming. Widespread deglaciation did not begin until atmospheric CO₂ rose around 18,000 years ago.

At that pace the ice edge moved roughly a kilometer in a human lifetime. Slow, but not invisible: a person born beside the ice would have watched the margin withdraw a distance they could walk in fifteen minutes. Grandparents could have told the truth about where the ice used to stand.

Then it broke. The New England Varve Chronology records steady net retreat up the Hudson Valley between 18,000 and 14,700 years ago — and **at 14,700 the net retreat rate tripled**, coinciding exactly with the onset of the **Bølling–Allerød interstadial**, an abrupt hemispheric warming. Cosmogenic dating in the Adirondacks shows the same signature: accelerated ice thinning between about 15,400 and 13,900 years ago.

**Which places this hill precisely.** The Wisconsin ice left the Helderberg Plateau at approximately 14,700 years ago — not merely during the great deglaciation but at its hinge, in the exact millennium the retreat went from a crawl to a rout. This hilltop emerged into daylight at one of the sharpest climate transitions of the last hundred thousand years.

It was not over. The **Younger Dryas** (12,900–11,700 years ago) slammed the door shut again — a sudden return to near-glacial cold during which parts of the ice sheet stabilized or readvanced. The Holocene, the interglacial still running, began 11,700 years ago. The Laurentide itself hung on in Labrador into the Holocene, finally vanishing only around six to seven thousand years ago — which is to say the last of the ice that covered this hill outlived the invention of agriculture.

**What people were doing when this hill came out from under the ice.** The Helderberg Plateau went ice-free at 14,700 years ago; here is the same moment elsewhere on Earth.

- **The Americas were already occupied — just not here.** The Clovis-first model is dead; consensus now places people in North America at or before 15,000 years ago. **Monte Verde in Chile** dates to 18,500–14,500 years ago, with wooden artifacts, hearths, and edible seaweed carried more than fifteen kilometers inland. **Cooper's Ferry, Idaho** dates to about 16,000. People had reached Patagonia before anyone stood on this hilltop, because this hilltop was under a mile of ice.
- **Europe, deep in the Magdalenian.** Lascaux and Altamira were painted around 17,000 years ago — meaning that when this hill emerged, those caves were already older to their contemporaries than the Roman Empire is to us.
- **The oldest dog anyone agrees on.** The **Bonn-Oberkassel dog** in Germany, about 14,200–14,300 years ago, was buried alongside a double human burial — and its bones show pathologies that would have been fatal without prolonged human care. Someone nursed a sick dog through an illness, and then buried it with their dead, at almost exactly the moment the ice let go of this hill.
- **The Levant was inventing staying put.** The **Natufians**, the first sedentary hunter-gatherers, were building stone dwellings and harvesting wild cereals. Shell beads from Kebara Cave on Mount Carmel, about 15,000 years old, were dyed red with a colourant made from the roots of madder-family plants — the oldest reliable evidence of organic plant-based pigment anywhere.
- **Pottery already existed in East Asia**, in China and in the incipient Jōmon of Japan, tens of thousands of years before it appeared in the Near East.

Agriculture was still three thousand years away. Writing was ten thousand years away. And the ground under Albany Hill had just seen sunlight for the first time in tens of thousands of years.

**~13,000 years ago — the first people and the last megafauna.** On the raw post-glacial tundra came mastodons (the Cohoes Mastodon, unearthed in 1866 from a glacial pothole downstream and now in the New York State Museum in Albany, is the region's celebrity), giant beavers the size of black bears, stag-moose, caribou, and muskox. And Paleoindian hunters, whose chert quarries and workshops survive along this very escarpment — **West Athens Hill** in Greene County is one of the most significant Paleoindian sites in the Northeast, a hilltop where people came specifically for the stone. Then the megafauna vanished, within a geological instant of human arrival, and the forest closed in behind them.

**~400 years ago to now — the fast erasures.** Chestnut, elm, passenger pigeon, wolf, cougar, elk, lynx, beaver, turkey, deer, and old-growth forest itself all went in a blink; some returned, most did not. New York was roughly three-quarters deforested by 1880 and is roughly two-thirds forested again today — a full ecological round trip inside six generations. Which is the shortest and strangest layer of all: the one still moving.

## WINTER

### 1. Sun Dogs Over Still Hill — January 1–15

- The deepest cold of the year settles in; on the most brutal mornings, ice crystals suspended in the air refract the low sun into *parhelia* — sun dogs — twin bright smudges flanking the sunrise over the eastern valley.
- On the coldest still nights, light pillars rise from the valley lights below — vertical columns painted on falling plate crystals drifting in calm air.
- The bitterest nights can produce a *cryoseism* (frost quake): the sharp crack of saturated ground freezing and splitting, easily mistaken for a falling limb.
- Black-capped chickadee (*Poecile atricapillus*) flocks work the gray dogwood thickets in mixed company with tufted titmice, white-breasted nuthatches, and downy woodpeckers — a winter foraging guild that travels together for shared vigilance against hawks.
- Beneath the snow, the *subnivean* zone — the hidden lattice of tunnels between snowpack and soil — holds a steady ~32°F while air temperatures plunge below zero, keeping meadow voles and short-tailed shrews alive and active all winter.
- The subnivean cast is worth separating, because "mouse" covers four very different animals. **Meadow voles** (*Microtus pennsylvanicus*) are blunt-nosed, short-tailed, tiny-eared grass specialists that live in the open field and cut the surface runways revealed at snowmelt; they are the single most important prey animal on this landscape and drive owl, fox, coyote, and weasel numbers. **White-footed mice** and **deer mice** (*Peromyscus leucopus* and *P. maniculatus*) are big-eyed, long-tailed, and semi-arboreal, the ones that get into buildings. **Southern red-backed voles** hold the mossy woods. **Short-tailed shrews** (*Blarina brevicauda*) are not rodents at all but insectivores — and they are **venomous**, one of very few venomous mammals on earth, with toxic saliva that paralyzes invertebrates and small prey so it can be cached alive.
- Shrews run a metabolism so extreme they must eat every few hours or die, and they cannot hibernate; some shrew species shrink their own skulls and brains by up to 20% over winter to reduce energy demand, then regrow them in spring — a phenomenon known as **Dehnel's effect**.
- White-footed mice are the principal reservoir for the Lyme bacterium in this region, and their abundance tracks the previous autumn's acorn crop. A heavy mast year produces a mouse boom the following summer, and a heavier tick-infection year the summer after — an oak-to-mouse-to-tick chain that runs on roughly a two-year lag.
- Red fox and coyote hunt the subnivean layer by ear — the head-cocked pause, then the high arcing pounce through the crust.
- **Chickadee.** The winter flock is a stable hierarchy of roughly six to ten birds — a dominant pair, subordinates, and floaters — holding a territory of about twenty acres, which means the flock working these thickets is likely *the* flock, the same individuals seen day after day. Rank determines feeder order and, in a hard winter, who survives.
- A chickadee weighs about the same as two nickels and must eat something on the order of its own body weight daily in deep cold. It survives the fifteen-hour night by **regulated nocturnal hypothermia**, deliberately dropping its body temperature by roughly 10–12°C in a sheltered cavity — a controlled, reversible chill that cuts the overnight fuel bill by a quarter or more — then shivering itself back up before dawn.
- The *chick-a-dee* call is a compositional signal, not a name. The number of terminal *dee* notes encodes predator threat, and the relationship is inverse to size: small, agile, maneuverable hunters that can chase a chickadee through branches — a sharp-shinned hawk, a screech-owl — draw the most *dees*, while a large hawk or a great horned owl draws fewer. This was demonstrated by Templeton, Greene, and Davis in *Science* in 2005, presenting fifteen live predator species and confirming with playback that other chickadees act on the information. Counting *dees* is genuinely reading a report on what is in the tree.
- **Titmouse.** The tufted titmouse is a recent arrival, not an old resident. It was a southern bird through the nineteenth century and expanded steadily north across the twentieth, reaching and then saturating upstate New York on the back of winter feeding stations and moderating winters. Anyone born early enough in these hills remembers when there were none.
- Titmice hold territory in family groups, and unusually among northern songbirds, some young stay with their parents through the winter rather than dispersing — so a titmouse pair with two extra birds in tow is often literally a family.
- For the Haudenosaunee, this is the threshold of Midwinter Ceremony — the turning of the ceremonial year, timed to when the Pleiades stand at their highest point at dusk; around 9 p.m. they sit directly overhead, a calendar in the sky that needs no reprinting.
- *Ghost:* This fortnight is the closest the year comes to the hill's default condition. For the great majority of the last hundred thousand years, this was simply what the landscape was — cold, treeless or nearly so, wind-scoured. The interglacial forest is the anomaly; January is the baseline briefly reasserting itself.
- *Health watch:* Bare crowns are readable crowns. Dieback shows plainest against a winter sky: a *stag-headed* tree — dead, antler-like limbs protruding above a shrunken living crown — is in advanced decline, and the pattern of death tells the cause. Dieback beginning at the top and working down and inward points to a root or vascular problem. Scattered dead branches through an otherwise full crown points to borers or canker. Dead branches only in the lower and inner crown is usually just shading, and normal.
- Wild grape (*Vitis riparia*, riverbank grape, and *V. aestivalis*, summer grape) is most visible now — thick shaggy-barked ropes running up into bare crowns, sometimes wrist-thick. Grape is native and valuable, but it is also one of the very few native plants that genuinely kills trees, by loading a crown until ice or wind takes the whole top out. A tree wearing a grape hat is a tree at structural risk.

- **Eastern red cedar turns colour in the cold**, and this is the month it shows. The foliage bronzes to a dull purple-brown in hard frost — a genuine seasonal pigment change, not damage — and greens again in spring. On a bare winter hillside the bronzed columns are unmistakable.
- **The blue berries are the winter larder.** They are not berries at all but fleshy cones, dusted with a pale waxy bloom, and they hold on the female trees through the whole winter. **Cedar waxwings take their name from this tree**, and a flock stripping a cedar in January is one of the reliable set-pieces of the season — birds passing fruit to one another along a branch before eating.
- Only the female trees carry them: red cedar is **dioecious**, separate male and female individuals, so half the cedars on a hillside will never fruit at all.
- **White spruce cones hang small, papery and pendant** at the branch tips, and the winter finches work them — **red crossbills and pine siskins in irruption years**, prying seeds out with bills evolved for exactly this. Red squirrels cut the cones down wholesale and pile them in middens at the base of the tree, and a spruce with a deep litter of stripped cone cores beneath it has a resident squirrel.

- **Foodways — applejack.** Cider left out in the cold separates: water freezes first and the alcohol concentrates in what stays liquid. **Freeze-distilling ("jacking") needs exactly the run of nights this microseason supplies**, and it was standard practice on Northeastern farms wherever there were apples and no still. The cider went up outdoors in a barrel, the ice was skimmed off repeatedly, and what remained was strong. It is one of the few preserving techniques that requires a specific climate rather than specific equipment, and this hill has the climate.
- **This is the deep root-cellar month.** Potatoes, turnips, rutabaga, carrots in damp sand, cabbages hung by the root, salt pork, dried apples, and crocks of sauerkraut still working slowly in the cold. **Nothing on a hill farm's winter table was harvested in winter** — every bit of it was decided in October.

### 2. Coyotes Court in the Blue Snow — January 16–31

- Eastern coyote breeding season peaks; nights fill with paired, conversational duets between mates rather than the yipping group choruses of autumn.
- Coyote tracks stitch the landscape in purposeful straight lines, often following packed human trails — they appreciate an easy path as much as anyone.
- **Fisher.** Neither a cat nor a fisher of fish — a very large arboreal weasel (*Pekania pennanti*) that was trapped out of most of New York by the early 1900s and has recolonized on its own. The track is the giveaway: five toes (unlike canines' four), often paired in a bounding **2–2 lope**, with a habit of running up one tree and down another that no fox or coyote can imitate.
- Fishers are among the very few animals that routinely kill **porcupines**, and the technique is specific: repeated frontal attacks to the face, circling the quills rather than defeating them, sometimes over half an hour, then feeding through the unquilled belly. Where fisher numbers rose across the Northeast, porcupine numbers fell measurably — a predator-prey relationship visible at landscape scale.
- **Ermine and long-tailed weasel** turn white in winter here, molting on day length rather than snowfall, which increasingly leaves them mismatched — conspicuous white animals on brown ground in a low-snow year. This is one of the most direct and visible ways a warming climate is killing individual animals on this hill.
- Fisher, ermine, long-tailed weasel, mink, and river otter are all mustelids present in this watershed; the family also includes the extirpated wolverine, gone from the Northeast for well over a century.
- Snow records everything now: the paired bounding gait of fisher, the delicate stitchery of deer mice, wing-swept craters where an owl connected with prey.
- **Crow.** American crows are in winter roosting mode, and the scale is easy to underestimate: birds forage in family groups by day across many miles, then converge at dusk on communal roosts that in the Capital Region can hold many thousands. Roosts favor urban and suburban sites — warmer, better lit, and safer from great horned owls, which are the main reason crows roost together at all. Watching the evening flight lines stream toward Albany is a reliable January–February spectacle.
- Crows recognize individual human faces, hold grudges for years, and transmit hostility toward specific people to birds that never had the original encounter — established by John Marzluff's masked-researcher experiments at the University of Washington. The corollary is that they also recognize kindness, and that a crow's opinion of a person is durable and socially contagious.
- Crow numbers crashed with the arrival of West Nile virus around 1999; corvids are exceptionally susceptible, and regional declines approached half the population before a partial recovery. The abundance visible today is a rebound, not a baseline.
- **Raven vs. crow**, which the bare season makes easy: the raven is a third larger with a heavy Bowie-knife bill, shows a **wedge-shaped or diamond tail** in flight against the crow's squared fan, soars and tumbles on flat wings where a crow rows steadily, wears shaggy throat hackles that stand out when it calls, and delivers a deep resonant *kronk* rather than a *caw*. Ravens also play — sliding down snowbanks, dropping and catching sticks in midair — which crows essentially do not.
- Great horned owls — the year's earliest nesters — court in the black locust stands: the male's deep four-to-five-note hoots answered by the female's higher, raspier reply.
- Daylight lengthens visibly; by month's end the hill gains nearly two minutes of light per day, and chickadees respond first, testing the two-note *fee-bee* spring song on any sunny morning above 20°F.
- Black locust pods rattle on the branch; gray dogwood stems glow oxblood-red against snow.
- *Marcescence* — the stubborn holding of dead leaves through winter — marks young beech and oak at the woodland edges, their pale leaves hissing in the wind.
- *Ghost:* The eastern wolf was gone from New York by the 1890s, hunted and bountied out. But the coyotes calling now are not the small western coyote — they are a larger animal carrying meaningful eastern-wolf ancestry, picked up as coyotes expanded through Ontario and hybridized with the remnant wolves they found there. The wolf is not entirely absent from these nights. It is simply speaking through a different throat.

### 3. Owl Eggs in the Bitter Bright — February 1–15

- Great horned owls are now incubating; the female sits through snowstorms with eggs beneath her, sometimes wearing a white cap of accumulated snow.
- This is often the coldest stretch of the year, when Still Hill's wind shadow matters most; drifts sculpt differently on the sheltered east slope than on exposed ridgelines.
- **Black bear cubs are born now**, in the den, to a mother who is dormant — blind, nearly hairless, and weighing under a pound, roughly 1/300th of the mother's weight, the smallest newborn relative to parent of any placental mammal. She nurses them through the rest of winter without eating, drinking, urinating, or defecating, recycling her own urea into protein. Bears also do not lose meaningful bone or muscle mass during months of immobility, a physiological trick medicine has been trying to reverse-engineer for decades.
- Bear denning here is casual by Adirondack standards — a brush pile, a rock overhang, an uprooted stump, sometimes a shallow depression under a fallen top. Southern-zone bears also den later and emerge earlier than northern ones.
- **Southern flying squirrels** (*Glaucomys volans*) are active every night of the winter and are almost certainly the most abundant squirrel on wooded land here, and the least seen. They are strictly nocturnal, glide up to 150 feet on the *patagium* (the skin membrane between wrist and ankle), steering with the tail as a rudder, and they **huddle communally in winter cavities** — sometimes a dozen or more birds' worth of body heat stacked in one hollow. A flashlight on a feeder at 10 p.m. in February is the way to confirm them.
- The northern flying squirrel (*G. sabrinus*), the boreal species, has been retreating northward and upslope as the southern species expands — partly climate, partly a parasitic nematode the southern squirrel tolerates and the northern one does not.
- On thaw days above freezing, snow surfaces near tree bases show what looks like spilled pepper: snow fleas (springtails), surfacing by the thousands to feed on algae and fungal spores, powered by a natural antifreeze protein — the year's first visible invertebrate stirring.
- Woodpecker drumming begins in earnest — pileated, hairy, downy — each species with its own cadence, territories declared in percussion on standing dead locust; drumming functions as song.
- **Great horned owl.** The earliest nester in the Northeast by a wide margin, and the reason is prey timing: incubating in February means the young fledge in late spring into a landscape stuffed with naive juvenile rabbits, squirrels, and songbirds. The owl builds nothing — it appropriates old red-tailed hawk, crow, or heron nests, or settles into a broken-topped snag or rock ledge. Any conspicuous stick nest still occupied in February is an owl's, not a hawk's.
- The species is functionally the apex nocturnal predator here and takes skunk routinely — owls have almost no sense of smell, and a great horned nest often reeks of it. It also kills other raptors and owls, which is precisely why crows mob it with such fury and why crows roost communally in winter.
- Female great horned owls are noticeably larger than males but have the *higher-pitched* voice — a reliable way to sex a duetting pair by ear, and backwards from what most people assume.
- **Raven.** Ravens nest even earlier than the owls and favor exactly this terrain: cliff ledges and quarry faces, which the Helderberg escarpment supplies abundantly. Nest-building and courtship flight — paired soaring, synchronized rolls, and the spectacular half-roll where a bird flies briefly upside down — run through February.
- **Screech-owl.** The eastern screech-owl comes in two genetically fixed color morphs, rufous and gray, which are not age or sex differences. Gray predominates toward the north, and the leading explanation is thermal: gray-morph birds survive severe winters at higher rates, so cold hills select for gray. Its voice is not a screech but a descending whinny or a long even trill — a small sound easy to mistake for a mammal.
- Sunrise now visibly migrates north along the eastern horizon; a fixed landmark (a particular tree, a far ridge notch) turns the hill itself into a solar observatory.
- *Ghost:* Common ravens were effectively driven out of this part of New York by the nineteenth century, surviving only in the Adirondack and Catskill wilds. They have returned within living memory, spreading back down the escarpment, and their croaking now carries over Helderberg farmland that had not heard it in over a century. A rare ghost walking back into the room.
- *Health watch:* Hemlock woolly adelgid is checkable now — roughly November through May is when the woolly stage is most visible. Turn a hemlock twig over and look where each needle joins the stem: the insect appears as tiny white masses like specks of wool or the ends of cotton swabs. Sap-clean twigs are good news. New York is releasing predator beetles against the adelgid, but detection on individual properties still happens one twig at a time.
- **Garden:** onion seeds started indoors now through early March get the long head start bulbing onions need at this latitude; seed catalogs and germination tests belong to this season.

- **Red cedar releases the first pollen of the year**, often while snow is still on the ground. On a mild bright afternoon the male trees smoke — puffs of gold lifting off the foliage at a touch or in a gust — weeks before anything else on the hill flowers. For anyone who reacts to it, cedar is the opening of the allergy year, and it opens it in February.
- **Cedar-apple rust galls are visible now as hard brown knobs** on the cedar twigs, walnut-sized and warty, entirely inert-looking. They spend the whole winter like this and will not do anything until the warm rains of spring, when they swell and extrude the orange gelatinous horns already described in May. **February is when to find and remove them**, while they are dry, obvious and harmless.

- **Skunk cabbage is the first flower of the year, and it makes its own heat to do it.** The hooded spathe — mottled maroon and green, shaped like a fist — pushes up through frozen seep ground now, and **it is thermogenic**: metabolising stored starch to hold its interior as much as 20°C above the surrounding air, for two weeks or more. **It melts its own opening through snow and ice.** In a February seep, a ring of bare wet ground around a purple hood is the plant heating the world immediately around it.
- **The heat is advertising.** It volatilises a carrion smell that draws out the earliest flies and small beetles, and offers them warmth as payment — a genuine reward in February, when nothing else on the hill is above freezing. **It is the only pollination system on the property that runs on heat rather than sugar.**
- **Sensitive fern's fertile fronds are standing black and rigid** through all of this. The plant is dimorphic: the green sterile fronds died at the first frost, but the separate fertile stalks — stiff, upright, hung with hard bead-like segments — persist all winter and release their spores next spring. **They are one of the few things still standing upright in a February wetland**, and they mark exactly where the wet ground is under the snow.

### 4. Sap Stirs Under Bark — February 16–28

- The first freeze-thaw cycles arrive — nights in the teens, days nudging 40°F — and sugar maples begin moving sap; runs on the hill start a week or more behind the valley sugarbushes and last correspondingly later into March, the hill's cold for once an asset.
- Maple saplings in old-field openings are too young to tap, but on a warm afternoon their bark carries the faint wet smell of a tree waking.
- For the Haudenosaunee this is the season of the maple's gift, honored with ceremony — the maple as leader of the trees, first to stir and first to give.
- Hazel catkins — formed the previous fall and hanging rigid all winter — begin to soften almost imperceptibly.
- Striped skunks emerge from winter dormancy (not true hibernation but *torpor*), leaving meandering tracks and occasional musk on thaw nights; raccoons resume travel the same nights.
- **Crow nesting** begins in late February and March, and crows are **cooperative breeders**: offspring from previous years frequently remain on the natal territory as helpers, defending it and feeding their younger siblings. A crow family group of five or six birds moving through a field together is usually parents plus grown children who have not left, and some helpers stay for several years.
- Barred owl courtship peaks now, and it is the least dignified sound in the winter woods — pairs escalate from the standard *who-cooks-for-you, who-cooks-for-you-all* into cackling, gurgling, monkey-like caterwauling that startles anyone hearing it for the first time. Barred owls have expanded and largely hold the wetter, lower woods, while great horned owls hold the open edges; where they overlap, the great horned kills the barred, and territories sort accordingly.
- The first turkey vultures may tilt over the valley on a warm southerly day, though the hilltop still reads as full winter.
- **Gray squirrel** mating chases peak — the spiraling, chattering pursuits of three or four males after one female through the bare canopy. Gray squirrels have two breeding seasons, this one and a summer one, and the winter litters are born into the coldest weeks of the year in leaf nests (*dreys*) or, preferably, tree cavities.
- **Opossum.** North America's only marsupial, and this hill is very near the northern edge of what it can survive. Opossums do not hibernate, do not truly den up, and forage through winter on a metabolism too low to generate much heat — which is why nearly every northern opossum has frostbitten ear margins and a stubbed tail. Their range has crept north with warming winters and human food subsidies within the last century; they were not here in colonial times.
- The tick claim is worth correcting because it is everywhere: a widely repeated figure of ~5,500 ticks eaten per week came from a 2009 lab study of caged animals. In 2021, Hennessy and Hild examined the stomach contents of 32 wild opossums and, in a review of 23 further diet studies, found **not a single tick or tick fragment**. Opossums are excellent animals for many reasons — near-immunity to rabies owing to low body temperature, resistance to pit viper venom, prodigious scavenging — but tick control is not among them.
- *Ghost:* Sugaring here is older than metal. Before kettles, sap was concentrated by dropping fire-heated stones into wooden or bark troughs, and by letting the cold do the work — freezing sap overnight and discarding the ice, which is nearly pure water. The technology changed; the fortnight did not.
- **Apple & peach:** dormant pruning season, and it runs from now until buds swell. Pruning in hard cold is fine and pruning in wet weather is not — fire blight and canker fungi both travel in moisture, so dry days are the rule, ideally cold and dry. Any dormant oil or copper for peach leaf curl goes on before bud swell; once the buds move, the window has closed for the year.
- **Garden:** onion starts continue indoors; leek and celery seeds join them late in the window.

## SPRING

- **Oaks are legible in winter, and the bark tells the species.** Chestnut oak carries the deepest, most massively ridged bark of any oak here — long vertical mountain ranges with V-shaped valleys, unmistakable at a glance on a rocky ridge. White oak is pale ashy grey in flat plates. Red oak carries the distinctive shiny grey "ski trails" running down between the furrows, as if someone had drawn straight lines on the trunk.
- **Shagbark hickory needs no explanation in February.** Long plates of bark curl away from the trunk at top and bottom, giving the tree a permanently dishevelled look — and those loose plates are roosting habitat. Several bat species, including the northern long-eared, spend summer days pressed between shaggy bark and trunk, so a shagbark is bat infrastructure as surely as punky dead wood is chickadee infrastructure.
- **Hop hornbeam still holds its fruit** — clusters of papery, inflated sacs like small green hops, bleached to tan by now and rattling in wind. The tree is also called ironwood, and deservedly: the wood is among the hardest in North America and was used for tool handles, sled runners and mallets when nothing else would serve.
- **Marcescence** is the winter oak habit worth naming: young oaks and beeches hold dead brown leaves all winter rather than shedding them, releasing them only as the new buds push in spring. On a hillside in February the brown patches of held leaves map exactly where the young oaks are.

- **Foodways — the sugar season, and whose it was first.** Maple sugaring is an **Indigenous technology** that Europeans learned rather than invented. Haudenosaunee and Algonquian peoples across the northern woodlands were tapping maples and reducing sap long before contact, using heated stones and clay or bark vessels, and freeze-concentration on cold nights — the same principle as applejack, working in reverse. The Haudenosaunee **Maple Ceremony** marks this moment as the year's first thanksgiving, before anything else has stirred.
- **Sugar was the point, not syrup.** Before cheap cane, maple was reduced all the way to hard cakes and grained sugar, which kept indefinitely and travelled. Syrup as the primary product is a modern preference built on tin cans and refrigeration.
- **And this hill runs late.** Sap moves when nights fall below freezing and days rise above it, and at 1,200 feet that alternation starts and ends behind the valley — the season here opens later and can run later, sometimes into the last days of March.

### 5. Hazel Catkins Loosen — March 1–15

- Hazelnuts stage the hill's first bloom, weeks before anything else dares: male catkins elongate from stiff gray fingers into loose golden tassels shedding pollen on dry breezy days, while female flowers open as nearly invisible crimson tufts at the bud tips — hand-lens territory, and worth the trouble.
- Hazel is wind-pollinated and frost-hardy, blooming in what is still functionally winter — a botanical act of faith from a shrub tough enough to resprout after repeated deer defoliation.
- Red-winged blackbirds (*Agelaius phoeniceus*) return to the valley wetlands and Basic Creek margins, their *konk-la-ree* audible from below before any have claimed the hilltop itself.
- Male red-wings arrive two to four weeks ahead of the females — an entirely separate migration — and spend that gap fighting over territory boundaries, so the earliest songs of the year are being sung to an audience of rivals rather than mates.
- The red epaulet is a switchable badge, not fixed plumage. A territorial male flares it to blazing scarlet while song-spreading; the same bird crossing another male's territory can cover it almost completely with the surrounding feathers and pass as a subordinate. Experiments blackening the patch cause males to lose their territories outright — the badge does the work the fighting would otherwise require.
- Mourning cloak butterflies — which overwinter as adults tucked into bark crevices — flap raggedly through the woods on the first 50°F afternoon, the year's first butterfly, drinking sap runs rather than nectar.
- Mud season begins; frost leaves the ground unevenly — south-facing patches first, the Still Hill shadow line last — and drainage patterns write themselves plainly in any dirt lane.
- **Eastern chipmunks** emerge from their winter torpor now on mild days — not true hibernation but a series of deep torpor bouts, waking every few days to eat from a larder stored in the burrow rather than living off fat. A chipmunk sleeps *on top of* its food supply, in a chamber where the seed pile is periodically excavated from beneath the animal.
- Bears begin leaving dens in March, males first, females with cubs last — and they emerge at the leanest, hungriest moment of the year into a landscape with almost no natural food. This is the peak window for bird feeder and garbage conflict, and the DEC guidance to pull feeders by late March is not a formality.
- **Woodchucks** are the region's only true hibernator among the large rodents — body temperature falling to near 40°F, heart rate to a handful of beats per minute — and males emerge weeks before females to locate burrows and begin the breeding season, which is the actual origin of the Groundhog Day observation.
- *Ghost:* Frost-heave is an excellent archaeologist. Mud season pushes stone to the surface, and on this escarpment some of what surfaces is chert — the flint that gave the Kanien'kehá:ka their name, and the reason Paleoindian hunters climbed hills like this one thirteen thousand years ago carrying nothing but the intention to knap. A flake with a conchoidal fracture and a feathered edge is not a natural shape.
- Bare-stem season is the honest season for telling **Virginia creeper** from **poison ivy**, since both climb the same trunks and both are leafless now. Virginia creeper climbs with branched tendrils tipped in small adhesive pads, so its stem lies flat and tidy against the bark. Poison ivy climbs with dense reddish-brown aerial rootlets that make the vine look furry or hairy — the classic hairy rope. When the leaves return, the count settles it: creeper carries five leaflets, poison ivy three.
- **Garden:** tomato seeds start indoors mid-March (6–8 weeks before the ~May 20–25 transplant gate); peppers, if grown, start even earlier in this window. Breadseed poppies surface-sow now directly onto late snow or bare frozen ground — they require cold stratification and light to germinate, and freeze-thaw honeycombing pulls the seed into perfect contact.

### 6. Redwings Climb the Hill — March 16–31

- Blackbirds and grackles finally claim the hilltop, a week or more after their valley kin.
- The vernal equinox passes (March 19–21), bringing the fastest daylight gain of the whole year — nearly three minutes per day.
- Spring peepers begin in the valley's low wet spots; the hill's cold hollows stay silent a while longer — a perfect annual demonstration of the microclimate lag.
- **Porcupine.** Slow, nearsighted, and entirely committed to a defense that works: roughly 30,000 quills, each a modified hair tipped with microscopic backward-facing barbs that make extraction agonizing and drive the quill deeper with muscle movement. Porcupines cannot throw quills — the tail slaps, and quills detach on contact.
- Porcupine winter feeding sign is unmistakable and worth learning: irregular pale patches of stripped bark high in hemlocks and sugar maples, coarse nipped twigs on the snow beneath, and a **conical pile of sawdust-like scat** at the base of a den tree or rock crevice, sometimes years deep. They den communally in cold snaps and can girdle and kill a favored tree over successive winters.
- Porcupines crave sodium to a degree that shapes their behavior — gnawing plywood, axe handles, outhouses, road-salted surfaces, and anything human hands have sweated on.
- Late March opens the hill's finest *crepuscular* theater: American woodcock begin the sky dance on mild evenings — the nasal, insistent *peent* from field edges, then the twittering spiral flight three hundred feet up on whistling wing feathers, and the silent zigzag fall.
- Eastern phoebes return to check previous years' nest sites; turkey vultures become daily.
- Female red-winged blackbirds arrive now and look like an entirely different species — heavily streaked dark brown, sparrow-like, with no red anywhere. Half of all reports of "a strange large streaky sparrow in the wet field" are female red-wings.
- Red-wings are among the most polygynous birds in North America: a single male may hold five to fifteen nesting females on a good territory. Yet genetic studies find that roughly a quarter to a half of nestlings are sired by *other* males, so the territory holder is often provisioning a substantial number of another bird's young. The showiest male on the cattails is running a considerably less exclusive operation than he appears to be.
- **Turkey.** Gobbling begins as daylight lengthens — the sound carries a mile or more on a still morning, and gobbles are delivered from the roost before fly-down. Toms strut in full display with tail fanned, wings dragging, feathers erect, and the bare head flushing between red, white, and vivid blue as blood flow shifts with excitement. The **snood** — the fleshy appendage over the bill — elongates dramatically during display, and hens have been shown to prefer long-snooded males.
- Wild turkeys here are the eastern subspecies, *Meleagris gallopavo silvestris*, and their presence is the most complete wildlife restoration in New York's history. Unregulated hunting and the clearing of about 75% of the state left the last wild birds recorded in the 1840s. A century later, around 1948, turkeys from a remnant Pennsylvania population walked north across the border into western New York on their own as abandoned hill farms returned to woods. Game-farm releases in the 1950s failed — pen-raised birds could not evade predators — and it was live trap-and-transfer of genuinely wild birds, begun in 1959, that repopulated the state. Every turkey on this hill descends from birds that walked back.
- Strategic invasive window: Morrow's honeysuckle breaks bud earlier than any native shrub, and for these two weeks it stands out in the gray woods as if highlighted — the easiest time of year to mark, cut, or pull it.
- *Ghost:* **The passenger pigeon.** This is the fortnight they came. Flocks a mile wide and hours long, moving up the Hudson corridor and over these hills — an estimated three to five billion birds, possibly the most abundant bird that ever lived, and the loudest thing in the eastern sky. They were gone from New York by about 1900 and gone entirely on September 1, 1914. Every March here is a March missing its central event, and nothing has grown into the space they left. There is a local relic of them, and it is a strange one: passenger pigeon bones have been recovered from clay deposits in Diddly Cave near Clarksville, alongside those of varying hare, in a karst system carved by meltwater beneath the retreating ice. The bird that vanished a century ago is preserved in a cave that records the end of the last glaciation — two of this calendar's ghosts in the same handful of sediment.
- **Garden (heat-lovers):** spilanthes / toothache plant (*Acmella oleracea*) starts indoors now, roughly eight weeks ahead of the late-May transplant gate. It is a frost-tender tropical annual from Brazil with no winter hardiness whatsoever at this elevation, so it is a container-or-transplant crop every single year.
- **Garden:** onion starts harden off late in the window on mild days; brassica and lettuce seeds can start indoors for the earliest transplants.

- **Speckled alder flowers before anything leafs out**, long purple-brown male catkins loosening and lengthening in the wet margins on the first warm days. The name comes from the pale horizontal **lenticels** speckling its bark — breathing pores, visible year-round and the easiest winter identification.
- **It fixes its own nitrogen, and not the way legumes do.** Alder partners with **Frankia**, a filamentous bacterium, in an **actinorhizal** symbiosis — the same arrangement as bayberry. **This is why alder colonises raw wet mineral ground where nothing else will start**, and why it improves the soil for everything that follows it.
- **The Big Night belongs to this microseason.** On the first rainy evening when air temperature holds above about 40°F, **spotted salamanders and wood frogs move en masse to the vernal pools**, often over snow and frozen ground, sometimes in a single night. The salamanders return to the pool they hatched in, and the whole migration can be over before midnight.
- **Wood frogs spend the winter frozen solid**, heart stopped, up to two-thirds of their body water turned to ice, protected by glucose flooded into their cells as cryoprotectant. **They thaw and are calling within days.** The sound is a low clacking, more like ducks than frogs, and it is one of the first animal voices of the year.
- **Fishless seasonal water is the whole point.** A pool that dries by late summer cannot hold fish or bullfrogs, which is precisely why wood frogs and spotted salamanders require it — and why a shallow spring pool is worth more than a permanent pond.

### 7. Woodcock Sky Dance — April 1–15

- Peak woodcock display — the hill's signature spring ritual — runs roughly 45 minutes after sunset on evenings above 40°F with light wind.
- Wood frogs, explosive breeders, gather in ephemeral pools for a brief clacking-duck chorus, often with ice still rimming the water; spring peepers reach the hill's cold hollows by mid-month, a full week behind the valley.
- Coltsfoot — dandelion's earlier, scalier cousin — blooms along lane edges and disturbed ground; bloodroot unfurls in richer woods-edge soil, each white flower wrapped in its own leaf like a hand around a candle.
- Peak turkey display. Toms gather harems and fight in earnest — spurs and wing-beating, sometimes for many minutes — while hens begin slipping away to nest. Dominant toms do most of the breeding, and satellite males often hang at the edges of a display without ever mating; brothers sometimes strut cooperatively, one displaying while the other never breeds, which pays off genetically through shared ancestry.
- Northern saw-whet owls move north through in April — a bird the size of a soda can that is far more common than almost anyone realizes. Their spring advertising call is a monotonous, mechanical *toot-toot-toot* at roughly two notes per second, easily mistaken for a truck backing up or a distant beeping electronic. Migration banding across the Northeast has repeatedly caught more saw-whets in a single autumn night than most birders see in a lifetime of looking.
- Blue jays turn quiet and inconspicuous now, which is the reverse of their reputation. Nesting jays are secretive — they stop screaming, slip in and out of the nest tree silently, and a pair can raise a brood in a dooryard without being noticed at all.
- The chorus of returning birds builds daily: field sparrows trilling like a dropped ping-pong ball in the old field, brown thrashers in the multiflora tangles (the one grudging service that invasive provides), yellow-bellied sapsuckers testing every resonant surface.
- White-tailed deer, gaunt and shedding, hit young woody plantings hardest now — the hungriest gap of their year; this is the season tree-tube protection on young hazels matters most, and the window to check tubes for frost heave.
- The reason late-winter deer starve with full stomachs is microbial: a deer's rumen flora is seasonally specialized, and an animal adapted over months to woody browse cannot immediately digest rich spring growth or supplemental corn. Emergency feeding in March routinely kills deer outright — *rumen acidosis* — which is why the practice is discouraged and, in New York, illegal.
- Bucks have cast their antlers by now, and the sheds are on the ground for a very short window — mice, squirrels, and porcupines gnaw them for calcium and phosphorus and can consume a shed entirely within a season. A shed with tooth-grooves along its edges has already been in the rodent economy.
- Yearling bears — last year's cubs, newly evicted by their mothers ahead of the breeding season — disperse now, and these naive young animals account for most unexpected bear appearances in odd places.
- *Ghost:* Coltsfoot is itself a ghost of arrival — a European medicinal plant carried over deliberately for cough remedies (its genus, *Tussilago*, means "cough dispeller") and long since escaped into every disturbed roadside in the Northeast. Half the earliest blooms of this hill's spring are immigrants with paperwork.
- **Garden:** as soon as soil is workable and ~40°F, the cold-soil crew direct-sows — radishes, spinach, and the first lettuce mid-to-late month; onion sets and hardened starts go out late in the window. Catnip and cilantro seeds can start indoors.

- **Foodways — the hungry gap, and the spring tonic.** This is the leanest fortnight of the agricultural year: the root cellar is nearly empty, nothing is ripe, and the stored food has been eaten or has spoiled. **Every culture that farmed this hill answered it the same way — with the first green things**, eaten as much for the change as for the nutrition.
- **Ramps (wild leeks)** come up in rich shaded ground now, and were dug by Indigenous peoples across this region long before anyone else arrived; the Irish and Germans who came later slotted them straight into the same role as wild garlic and wild onion at home. **Dig sparingly — a ramp takes five to seven years to reach harvestable size, and whole patches have been eliminated by commercial digging.** Cutting a single leaf and leaving the bulb is the low-impact method.
- **Dandelion greens, nettle, watercress, dock and violet leaves** were all spring-tonic foods here. Nettle in particular carried the same reputation in Irish, German and Indigenous practice independently — a bitter green taken deliberately after a winter of salt meat and starch.
- **Fiddleheads** of ostrich fern uncoil in damp bottomland shortly after this, and remain the one genuinely commercial wild vegetable of the Northeast.

- **Spicebush flowers before its leaves**, a haze of tiny yellow-green clusters along bare grey twigs in the wet understory — one of the earliest woody plants to bloom here, and easy to mistake for a yellow mist at a distance. **Every part of it is aromatic**: crush a twig and the smell is somewhere between allspice and citrus. The leaves and twigs made a widely used tea, and the dried red fruit was used as a substitute for allspice.
- **Marsh marigold opens in the seeps** — deep glossy yellow, five to nine petal-like sepals, in the wettest ground it can find. It is a buttercup relative and **toxic raw**; traditional preparation involved boiling through several changes of water, and it is not worth attempting casually.
- **Cinnamon fern fiddleheads come up wrapped in wool.** The dense cinnamon-coloured fuzz is one of the sights of an April wetland, and **hummingbirds strip it for nest lining** — arriving in exactly these weeks and building with material this plant is producing at the same moment. Two species keeping the same schedule for entirely different reasons.
- **Skunk cabbage now sends up its leaves**, after the flower rather than with it — huge, bright, cabbage-like, expanding fast enough to watch. **They will be gone entirely by August**, collapsing into slime and leaving almost nothing behind, so the plant's whole above-ground year runs February to July.
- **Its roots pull it downward.** Skunk cabbage has **contractile roots** that shorten each year and drag the crown deeper, which is why an old plant sits so far down that digging one out is close to impossible.

### 8. Peepers in the Cold Hollows — April 16–30

- Full amphibian chorus at last, and the hill greens from the bottom up — literally, since invasive honeysuckle leafs out first, then multiflora rose, well before the natives.
- The invaders' head start is precisely why they are invasive — the technical term is *extended leaf phenology*, stealing light in both shoulder seasons — and it keeps them easy to map for another two weeks.
- Sugar maple flowers open — easily missed, but visible as chartreuse tassels dangling before the leaves, feeding the earliest native bees.
- Serviceberry (shadbush) blooms white at valley woodland edges now; the hill's own follows at the very end of the month.
- Ruby-crowned kinglets and the first warbler scouts — pine, palm, yellow-rumped — move through the dogwood thickets.
- Chickadees excavate their own nest cavities rather than only occupying found ones, chiseling into soft punky wood — dead birch, rotting stubs of gray dogwood and locust — and carrying the chips a distance away before dropping them, so as not to advertise the site. Standing dead wood of small diameter is therefore chickadee infrastructure, not clutter.
- A cornered nesting chickadee performs the **snake display**: a sudden explosive hiss and lunge inside the dark cavity, evolved to convince a probing predator that something worse lives there.
- Titmice line their nests with mammal hair, and they do not wait for it to be shed — they pluck it directly from live animals, documented on woodchucks, dogs, raccoons, and sleeping people. A titmouse nest can be a shed-fur inventory of every mammal on the property.
- Hen turkeys are on the ground incubating now, in a shallow scrape against a log or in dense brush, and they sit exceptionally tight — relying entirely on camouflage and often not flushing until nearly stepped on. Nests are highly vulnerable to raccoon, fox, skunk, and coyote at this stage.
- Hermit thrush song begins in the damper woods: the spiraling flute-phrase that is arguably the finest sound the Northeast produces.
- **Bats** emerge from hibernation and reappear over the fields at dusk — and the count is a fraction of what it was. **White-nose syndrome began about twenty-five miles from this hill**: first photographed in a bat at Howe Cave in Schoharie County in February 2006, confirmed in surrounding caves the following winter, and now spread across most of the continent. The fungus *Pseudogymnoascus destructans* rouses hibernating bats repeatedly, burning the fat reserves that must last until spring. Little brown bat declines in New York hibernacula approached 90–100%.
- The species most likely overhead now are **big brown bats** (the most WNS-resilient, and the ones that use buildings) and the increasingly scarce little brown; northern long-eared and tri-colored bats are federally or state listed and functionally rare. A single bat can take thousands of insects nightly, so this is also a story about mosquito and moth populations.
- Beaver kits are born in May in the lodge; yearlings from the previous litter are still present, making a beaver colony a three-generation household.
- The last snow of the year remains entirely possible; the hill has seen accumulating snow in the first days of May, so nothing tender goes in the ground yet, whatever the valley garden centers are selling.
- *Ghost:* **The American elm.** Dutch elm disease arrived in the 1930s and by the 1970s had taken the tree that arched over nearly every village street and stood alone and vase-shaped in nearly every pasture in the Northeast. Elms still sprout and grow to fifteen or twenty feet in hedgerows before the beetles find them — the species persists as a permanent adolescent, never again reaching the canopy.
- **Peach** blooms now, and this is the entire problem with peaches on this hill: pink flowers opening in late April, weeks before the last-frost gate closes around May 20–25. Peach flower buds are also killed outright at roughly −10°F to −15°F, temperatures a 1,200-foot Helderberg hilltop reaches in most winters. A peach here is a legitimate gamble rather than a crop — fruit perhaps one year in three or four, in the hardiest cultivars (Reliance is the hardiest widely available, with Contender, Madison, and Veteran behind it), and best sited mid-slope where cold air drains past rather than in a hollow where it pools.
- *Ghost:* **The bats.** Within living memory, a summer dusk on an open hilltop like this one meant dozens of bats working the field, and the loss is recent enough that many people remember it happening. What makes it unbearably local is the origin: white-nose syndrome entered North America at Howe Cave in Schoharie County, roughly twenty-five miles from this hill, first photographed in February 2006 — most likely carried in on the clothing of a caver returning from Europe, into a commercial cave visited by hundreds of thousands of people a year. Millions of bats have died across the continent since. This particular extinction event has a documented ground zero, and it is within an easy drive.
- **Garden:** beets, carrots, and more lettuce and spinach direct-sow now (2–4 weeks before last frost); cilantro direct-sows late in the window; basil starts indoors — it goes outside only when nights hold above 50°F.

### 9. Green Fire in the Honeysuckle — May 1–15

- The green wave finally crests the hill, a week to ten days behind the Hudson Valley — and once started it moves with astonishing speed, leaf-out compressing into days: chokecherry, gray dogwood, maple saplings, black locust last of all (locust is famously late; a bare locust in mid-May is healthy, not dead).
- Warbler migration peaks in these two weeks: ovenbirds shouting *teacher-teacher-teacher* from the woods floor, black-throated greens in the conifers, chestnut-sided warblers claiming exactly the shrubby second-growth this landscape specializes in.
- Ruby-throated hummingbirds return — reliably within a few days of May 10 at this elevation — and Baltimore orioles pass through the flowering treetops.
- **Hummingbird.** The ruby-throated (*Archilochus colubris*) is the only hummingbird that breeds east of the Mississippi — a single species carrying the entire eastern hummingbird niche, unlike the western states with a dozen. Anything unusual at an eastern feeder is therefore genuinely unusual.
- Many arrive by crossing the Gulf of Mexico nonstop — roughly 500 miles over open water, up to twenty hours of continuous flight, on a bird weighing about three grams that nearly doubles its body weight in fat beforehand. Others take the longer coastal land route. Both strategies exist in the same population.
- Males precede females by roughly a week to ten days, and the arrival date on this hill is strikingly consistent year over year despite the ten-day lag behind the valley.
- **The sapsucker connection** is the key to their timing here: hummingbirds arrive well before most nectar flowers open at 1,200 feet, and they survive the gap by feeding at the sap wells drilled by yellow-bellied sapsuckers — the neat horizontal rows of holes on birches, maples, and hemlocks. The sap runs about the same sugar concentration as flower nectar, and the wells trap insects besides. Hummingbird northward migration tracks sapsucker distribution closely enough that the woodpecker is effectively the hummingbird's advance infrastructure crew.
- Overnight, a hummingbird enters deep **torpor** — body temperature falling toward the ambient air, heart rate dropping from over a thousand beats per minute to a few dozen, the bird cold and unresponsive on its perch until it shivers itself back to operating temperature at dawn. On a cold May night on this hill, a hummingbird is effectively switched off.
- Morel season, such as it is on acidic soil, peaks near old apple trees and dying elms.
- Porcupines give birth to a single enormous **porcupette** — relative to the mother, one of the largest single offspring of any rodent — born with soft quills that harden within hours, and able to climb within days.
- The first chipmunk litter appears above ground; chipmunks produce two litters a year here, this one and a late-summer one.
- Bear breeding season opens in late May and runs through June. Bears practice **delayed implantation** — the fertilized egg floats unattached for months and only implants in late autumn, and only if the female has put on enough fat. A poor mast year therefore erases a pregnancy that already happened, months after the fact.
- Frost remains a live threat through the end of this microseason and into the next; the growing season is real but probationary.
- The Haudenosaunee ceremonial calendar honors the planting season now, and the traditional wisdom of waiting for the proper signs before planting reads on this hill as straightforward practical advice.
- *Ghost:* American ginseng, which grows in exactly this region's rich shaded slopes, has been dug for export to China continuously since the 1700s — one of colonial New York's earliest cash exports and one of its longest-running extractions. Wild populations across the Northeast are a fraction of what they were, and the plant's rarity is not natural scarcity but three centuries of steady removal. The chestnut-sided warbler is the mirror image: rare in Audubon's day, abundant now, because clear-cutting and farm abandonment manufactured exactly the scrubby habitat it needs. Some ghosts are made by taking; some are made by leaving.
- Apple bloom opens in the last days of this window — pink bud, then white — running about a week behind valley orchards. Old orchard remnants and wild seedling apples bloom alongside the chokecherries.
- *Health watch:* Cedar-apple rust announces itself now if it is present. It requires eastern red cedar within roughly a mile as its alternate host, and on the cedars the galls swell in wet spring weather into bizarre orange gelatinous horns; on the apples, bright orange spots follow on the leaves a few weeks later. Removing nearby cedars is the old prescription and rarely practical; resistant cultivars are the modern one.
- **Garden:** succession sowings of lettuce, radishes, beets, and carrots continue; potatoes go in; tomato starts harden off on mild days but stay under cover at night.

### 10. Chokecherry Snow — May 16–31

- Chokecherries bloom — dense white bottlebrush racemes humming with native bees, small flies, and the first butterflies of substance — and when the petals drop at month's end they fall like a second, kinder snow; apple and wild cherry join.
- This is the hill's floral crescendo; its scent on a warm evening is the year's best argument for the place.
- The last-frost gate finally closes, roughly May 20–25 — a week or more after Albany's average — opening the tender planting season.
- Fawns begin dropping in the last days of May, curled motionless in tall grass; tall grass deserves a walk-through before any mowing.
- Hummingbird nesting is done entirely by the female — males contribute nothing beyond mating and are actively driven off. The nest is a walnut-sized cup of plant down bound with **spider silk** and shingled in lichen, and the silk is what makes it work: the cup stretches as the two jellybean-sized nestlings grow. It is almost perfectly camouflaged as a bump on a downsloping branch, and finding one is a genuine achievement.
- Turkey poults hatch in late May and early June. They leave the nest within a day and follow the hen immediately, unable to fly for about ten days — the most dangerous stretch of a turkey's life, and the reason cold wet Junes produce weak year-classes. Poults feed almost entirely on insects for their first weeks, not seeds, so an unmown insect-rich field is turkey nursery habitat.
- Whip-poor-wills, once common on exactly this kind of brushy upland edge, have declined steeply across the region — their absence, or a single calling bird, is worth noting on a warm still night now.
- Snapping turtles haul out of valley wetlands to lay eggs in any workable gravel, including road shoulders and lane edges.
- A newborn fawn is nearly **scentless**, and its instinct is to lie flat and motionless rather than flee — so the doe deliberately leaves it alone for hours at a time, feeding at a distance to avoid drawing predators to the spot. A fawn found alone is virtually never abandoned, and picking one up is the single most common well-intentioned wildlife mistake made in this region.
- Does drive off their yearlings just before giving birth, which is why late May and early June produce so many confused young deer wandering into unlikely places.
- Fawns are spotted for camouflage in dappled light and lose the spots at three to four months, on their first molt.
- The dawn chorus peaks in these two weeks, beginning a bewildering ~4:30 a.m.: field sparrow, indigo bunting, common yellowthroat, chestnut-sided warbler — the old-field ensemble, all breeding, all loud.
- The Eta Aquariid meteors (peaking early May) yield to the quiet sky of early summer; Vega climbs the northeast after dusk, herald of the Summer Triangle.
- *Ghost:* The land itself was rented, not owned. Westerlo lay inside **Rensselaerswyck**, the Van Rensselaer patroonship, and the numbered farm lots that still structure property boundaries here were laid out by William Cockburn, hired to survey the West Manor across Rensselaerville, Berne, Knox, and Westerlo. Tenants held perpetual leases paying rent in wheat or labor, plus a "quarter-sale" fine of up to a quarter of the price if they ever sold. When the "Good Patroon" Stephen Van Rensselaer III died in 1839 and his estate demanded roughly $400,000 in uncollected back rent, the hilltowns revolted — the first mass meeting was held in Berne on July 4, 1839, and the **Anti-Rent War** that followed ran until the 1846 constitutional convention outlawed agricultural leases longer than twelve years. Every stone wall on this hill was built by someone who did not own the ground it stood on.
- *Ghost:* The hill was sold twice over, a century apart, and both times to someone from away. In 1866 it was leasehold ground under the Van Rensselaer manor. In 1983 it was bought as a two-hundred-acre-plus tract by **Patten Realty**, a Vermont-born land-sale company whose stated business was purchasing large tracts "from farmers unable to market it themselves," subdividing them, and reselling five- to twenty-acre pieces to city dwellers as recreational property. The lots here were surveyed in November 1983 and sold through 1984 to buyers from Flushing and the boroughs. Patten went public in 1985, was briefly the biggest gainer on the New York Stock Exchange, settled deceptive-marketing claims with five states in 1989, was profiled on *60 Minutes* in 1991, and is now — renamed Bluegreen — a timeshare subsidiary of Hilton Grand Vacations. The stone walls were built by a tenant; the lot lines were drawn by a company that now sells vacation points.
- *Ghost:* Bloom season is when abandoned homesteads announce themselves. Apple trees in improbable rows far from any road, lilacs blooming out of a woodlot, daylilies and myrtle in a rectangle of nothing, a depression in the ground lined with cut stone — these are cellar holes, and the Helderberg uplands are full of them. Farms here were marginal from the start, and after the Erie Canal opened in 1825 and cheap western grain flooded east, hill farms across upstate New York were walked away from by the thousands. The flowers kept the appointment anyway.
- Peak apple bloom, roughly May 15–25 at this elevation — and the pollination window that decides the whole autumn crop. Cold, wet, or windy bloom weather means few bee flights and a thin harvest regardless of how heavy the flowering looked.
- *Health watch:* The single most important thing that kills old field apples here is not a disease at all — it is shade. Apples are intolerant of being overtopped, and a tree that once stood open in a pasture is now being closed over by locust, maple, and dogwood. A declining old apple with a thin, one-sided crown and heavy dead wood is usually asking for light, not medicine. Bloom season is the easiest time to find them, because they announce themselves in white from a distance.
- **Garden:** tomatoes transplant out once the frost gate closes; corn and beans direct-sow when soil reaches ~60°F, typically the last days of May into early June; catnip transplants out. The Three Sisters — corn, beans, squash interplanted — is the Haudenosaunee polyculture built for exactly this moment: corn first, beans a week or two later to climb it, squash last to shade the soil.

## SUMMER

- **Oak flowering passes almost unnoticed and is worth catching.** Long yellow-green catkins hang in curtains from every oak as the leaves expand, and the pollen is wind-carried in quantity — the yellow film on standing water in late May is largely oak.
- **The two oak groups keep different clocks, and the difference is visible on the twig.** White oak group — white, chestnut and swamp white — ripens acorns in a single season, from flower in May to acorn in September. **Red oak group — red, black and scarlet — takes two full years**, so a red oak in May carries two crops at once: the tiny first-year acorns just set, and the previous year's now swelling toward autumn. Learning to see both on one twig is the fastest way to tell the groups apart.
- **Shagbark hickory leafs out among the very last**, later even than black locust, and unfolds enormous compound leaves in a rush once it starts. The buds are equally distinctive when they break — large, and the outer scales peel back like a flower before the leaf emerges.
- **Swamp white oak carries its acorns on long stalks**, unlike its relatives, which sit theirs tight against the twig. It is the easiest field mark on the species, and the peeling, papery bark on its young branches is the other.
- **Black gum leafs out late and glossy**, the leaves so polished they look wet. It is one of the longest-lived hardwoods in eastern North America — individuals over six hundred years old are documented — and it is worth planting on that basis alone.

- **Foodways — the Three Sisters go in now, and the timing is the teaching.** Corn, beans and squash are planted together: corn as the pole, beans climbing it and fixing nitrogen, squash spreading below to shade out weeds and deter animals with prickly vines. **The Haudenosaunee call them the Three Sisters and treat them as a single agricultural and nutritional unit** — corn and beans together supply complete protein, which neither does alone. It is intercropping worked out centuries before the word existed.
- **Planting waits on signs rather than dates**, and at this elevation that matters — oak leaves the size of a squirrel's ear is the widely cited marker, and it lands here in the last days of May, when the frost gate finally closes.
- **The Palatine Germans arrived on the Hudson for pine tar, not for farming.** Roughly 2,100 refugees from the Rhineland were shipped over in **1710** and settled at East and West Camp on the river, under contract to produce **naval stores — pitch, tar and turpentine — from the pines**. The scheme failed within a few years, and the Palatines dispersed west into the **Schoharie Valley**, immediately over the hills from here, and later into the Mohawk. **Their descendants are all through the Hilltowns**, and the surnames on the 1866 atlas carry them.

### 11. Locust Perfume — June 1–15

- Black locust blooms, and for roughly ten days the hill smells like a perfume counter: heavy white wisteria-like racemes dripping nectar, mobbed by bumblebees whose collective hum is audible from thirty feet.
- Locust honey is prized for a reason; standing under a flowering tree at midday settles the question.
- Locust, a nitrogen-fixing legume, quietly fertilizes acidic soil while it perfumes the air — the rare tree that pays rent twice.
- Gray dogwood sets buds; lavender fattens toward bloom.
- First fledglings appear — spot-breasted robins, stub-tailed chickadees begging with quivering wings — and the woods fill with the anxious *chip* notes of parenting.
- Turkey broods appear in the fields — a hen with a wake of poults, often two or three hens combining broods into a crèche, which improves collective vigilance. A brood that dwindles from twelve to four over a few weeks is a normal outcome, not a catastrophe.
- Crow families are feeding young with helpers in attendance, and the begging call of a fledgling crow is a strange nasal rattle wholly unlike an adult *caw*.
- Chickadee broods fledge in a single morning — six to eight young leaving the cavity in a rush — and the family stays tightly together for two to three weeks before the young disperse, often joining unrelated flocks by late summer. The flock in the thickets next January will not be this year's family.
- White-tailed does are nursing; bucks, in velvet, band into summer bachelor groups.
- Antlers in velvet are the fastest-growing tissue in the mammal kingdom — up to half an inch a day on a mature buck — built from a blood-rich, nerve-laden skin that makes them genuinely sensitive, and drawing so heavily on calcium and phosphorus that the buck temporarily pulls minerals from his own skeleton to build them.
- Gray squirrel and red squirrel litters are out of the nest. **Red squirrels** (*Tamiasciurus hudsonicus*) deserve separating from grays: smaller, louder, fiercely territorial, conifer-associated, and **larder hoarders** — they defend a single central cache (a *midden*, often a years-deep pile of cone scales) rather than scattering. They are also significant nest predators on songbirds.
- Bear breeding peaks; males range enormously now, and a bear crossing this hill in June is likely traveling rather than resident.
- Evenings stretch gloriously — usable light past 9 p.m. by mid-month; the sun now sets as far north along the Still Hill ridgeline as it ever will, a markable solstice waypoint.
- Luna moths, pale green and improbable, come to lit windows on warm nights.
- *Ghost:* Black locust is not originally from here. Its native range is the southern Appalachians and the Ozarks; it reached New York because people planted it — for fence posts that refuse to rot, for erosion control, for ship timbers and railroad ties — and then it spread on its own. Every locust stand on this hill is the descendant of a decision. Whether that makes it a native, a naturalized citizen, or a well-behaved invasive is a question the ecologists have not settled either.
- Petal fall on the apples, and the start of the fire blight window — the bacterium enters through open blossoms and succulent new shoots, and warm humid weather right after bloom is what sets it off.
- *Health watch:* Fire blight is unmistakable once known. Shoot tips blacken as if scorched by a blowtorch and then bend over into a tight hook — the *shepherd's crook* — and leaves stay attached rather than dropping. Cut well below the visible margin, at least eight to twelve inches into clean wood, on a dry day, sterilizing the blade between every cut. Do not fertilize an infected tree; soft fast growth is exactly what it feeds on.

- **Pitch pine puts up its candles** — pale upright new shoots at the branch tips, soft and rubbery before the needles spread. The needles come in **bundles of three**, which separates it cleanly from white pine's five and red pine's two, and the whole tree looks slightly unkempt compared with either.
- **It also sprouts needles straight out of its trunk bark**, which no other pine here does. Those epicormic tufts are the visible sign of a fire-adapted tree: pitch pine can resprout after being burned, broken or cut, which is why it persists on rock and sand barrens where every competitor eventually fails.
- **White spruce identifies itself by smell.** Crush the needles and they are frankly unpleasant — the old name is *skunk spruce* — and the needles are four-sided, rolling easily between finger and thumb, where a fir's flat needles will not roll. This is near the southern edge of its range; a white spruce here is a boreal species holding on at elevation.

- **Blue flag iris opens in the wet ground** — violet-blue with the yellow-and-white signal patch on the falls that guides bumblebees in. It is the native iris of Northeastern wetlands, and **the rhizome is toxic**, which distinguishes it sharply from the sweet flag it is sometimes confused with.
- **Tussock sedge builds the architecture of a wet meadow, slowly.** Each plant raises its own pedestal of accumulated root and old leaf bases, and over decades the tussocks stand a foot or more above the water between them. **The result is microtopography: dry tops and wet hollows within inches of each other**, which is why a tussock sedge meadow holds far more species than flat ground of the same size. Old tussocks are decades of growth, and cannot be replaced quickly once destroyed.

### 12. Dogwood Lace & Fireflies — June 16–30

- Gray dogwood blooms — flat cream-colored *cymes* (branched, flat-topped flower clusters) spread across the thickets like doilies — the most democratic bloom on the landscape, feeding an astonishing breadth of small native bees, wasps, beetles, and flies.
- Dogwood thickets are the hill's biodiversity engine; twenty minutes of watching one cyme reveals a dozen insect species.
- The summer solstice arrives (June 20–21): the year's longest days, the sun rising and setting at its northern extremes — and Still Hill's afternoon shadow at its annual minimum, the one season it taxes the hill least.
- The first fireflies rise from unmown grass in the last week of June, later than the valley's, and build nightly; different species flash different codes at different heights and hours — the low, slow J-stroke of *Photinus pyralis* earliest, others higher and later.
- An unmown field is a firefly nursery — the strongest ecological argument for lazy mowing ever devised.
- For the Haudenosaunee this season belongs to the strawberry — the first fruit, honored with its own thanksgiving — and wild strawberries ripen in old-field grass now.
- Bat pups are born in June in maternity colonies — females aggregate in hot attics, barns, and tree cavities while males roost separately. A single pup per year is typical, which is precisely why bat populations cannot rebound quickly from white-nose losses: a species with the reproductive rate of a small elephant does not recover on a rodent's timeline.
- Chipmunks are conspicuous now, and their loud repetitive *chip* and lower *chuck* calls are alarm signals, not chatter — the high *chip* generally for aerial threats, the *chuck* for ground predators, and a whole hillside can take up the relay for many minutes after a hawk passes.
- *Ghost:* The old field itself is the ghost. Gray dogwood, maple saplings, and locust colonizing open ground is *succession*, and what it is succeeding from is pasture. In the 1830s and '40s upstate New York went mad for Merino sheep, and these hills were shaved nearly bare and walled into paddocks to hold them. The wool market collapsed, the western railroads arrived, the farms emptied, and the forest began its long walk back uphill. Every thicket on this hill is one frame of a two-century time-lapse still running.
- Wild grape blooms now, and almost nobody notices it — the flower clusters are small, green, and hidden under leaves, but the scent on a warm still evening is startling: sweet, faintly spicy, and carrying a surprising distance. It is one of the great unnoticed smells of a Northeast June.
- Apples shed their *June drop* — a self-thinning of surplus fruitlets that looks alarming and is entirely normal, the tree balancing crop load against its own reserves.
- *Health watch:* Beech leaf disease is diagnosable in these two weeks and essentially invisible the rest of the year. Stand under a beech and look up at the leaves with the sky behind them: infected leaves show dark, thickened bands striping the tissue between the veins, an unmistakable corduroy shadow. Backlighting is required; the same leaf viewed from above often looks fine.
- **Garden:** garlic scapes curl and get snapped off to push energy into bulbs; succession lettuce moves to part shade as heat builds; cilantro bolts fast now — resowing every 2–3 weeks keeps a leaf supply.

- **Foodways — the strawberry, and the ceremony that goes with it.** Wild strawberries ripen small and intensely flavoured now in field edges and old pasture, weeks ahead of anything cultivated. **The Haudenosaunee Strawberry Ceremony** marks them as the first fruit of the year and the leader of the berries, and the strawberry drink made for it is one of the year's fixed observances — a thanksgiving tied to a ripening rather than to a calendar date, which is what this whole document is about.
- **This is also the start of the year's real work for preservers.** Everything from here to October arrives in a rush and must be dealt with the week it comes: strawberries now, then raspberries, then the currants and gooseberries the Germans and Irish both planted heavily, then everything at once in August.

### 13. Lavender & Clearwing — July 1–15

- Lavender blooms — a Mediterranean guest thriving improbably in the thin, sharp drainage of the hilltop — and summons the hill's most delightful insect: the hummingbird clearwing moth, a day-flying sphinx that hovers, sips, and darts so convincingly that first-time observers swear it is a baby hummingbird.
- Snowberry clearwings join; actual ruby-throated hummingbirds work the same patch, and the three-way confusion is a July institution.
- Hummingbird aggression peaks now. A single male will claim an entire flowering patch or feeder as territory and spend the day in high-speed chases, dive displays, and audible bill-clacking collisions — body mass around three grams and behavior scaled for a much larger animal. Two feeders placed out of sight of each other defeat a monopolist far more effectively than one large feeder.
- The male's gorget is not pigment but structural color — microscopic layered platelets in the feather barbules — so the throat reads flat black from most angles and detonates into scarlet only when the bird turns into the light at the correct angle. He aims it deliberately during display.
- Common milkweed blooms in the field margins, its scent carrying for yards; the first monarch eggs appear on leaf undersides. Milkweed is another biodiversity engine — one umbel at hot noon hosts a countable crowd of species.
- Hay-scent rises from the fields; black raspberries ripen in the multiflora tangles.
- Young birds are everywhere and everything is molting, hungry, and slightly disheveled.
- The Summer Triangle — Vega, Deneb, Altair — rules the evening sky directly overhead, the Milky Way running through it on moonless nights; the hill's elevation and dark eastern horizon make this the season for it.
- *Ghost:* **The American chestnut**, and this is its fortnight. Chestnut bloomed late — creamy catkins in early July, weeks after every frost risk had passed — which is exactly why it was the most reliable mast tree in the eastern forest, never once failing to the late freezes that regularly wiped out the oaks. It was one in four trees in much of the Appalachian and Hudson Valley forest, and the blight introduced around 1904 killed an estimated four billion of them within forty years. The root systems did not die. Chestnut still sprouts from thousand-year-old stumps in these woods, grows ten or twenty feet, gets found by the fungus, and dies back — over and over, for a century, in a landscape that has forgotten what its summers used to smell like. Learn the leaf: long, canoe-shaped, with big forward-hooked teeth. There are more of them out there than anyone expects.
- Virginia creeper (*Parthenocissus quinquefolia*) is at full leaf and feeding a hidden crowd: it is the larval host for several of the largest and most spectacular sphinx moths in the region — the Pandorus sphinx, the Achemon sphinx, and the Virginia creeper sphinx, all of them heavy-bodied, beautifully marbled, and the same family as the clearwings working the lavender by day. Big green hornworms on a creeper vine are not a problem to solve; they are next year's sphinx moths.
- *Health watch:* Mid-July is the standard forest-health crown assessment window — the canopy is fully out, and nothing has begun to senesce yet, so what looks thin now genuinely is thin. Judged from a distance, a healthy hardwood crown is dense enough to hide its own branches. Count roughly how much of the crown is missing, dead, or see-through, note it, and repeat in the same week next year. A single reading means little; a three-year trend means everything.

- **Buttonbush flowers as perfect white spheres**, an inch across, bristling with protruding styles like a pincushion — one of the strangest-looking natives here, and among the most heavily worked. **Bees, wasps, butterflies and hummingbirds all use it**, and it blooms in the gap after the spring shrubs and before the goldenrod. It grows with its feet in standing water and is one of the few woody plants that positively wants to be flooded.

### 14. Cicada Noon — July 16–31

- Dog-day cicadas begin — the rising power-line whine from the treetops that is the true anthem of high summer — joined at night by the first katydids scratching *katy-did, katy-didn't* from the canopy.
- The year pivots here, audibly: bird song collapses as breeding ends and molt begins, and insects take over the soundscape entirely.
- Owls fall silent through midsummer — the woods can seem owl-less in July and are not. Calling resumes in earnest with autumn territorial disputes.
- Young hummingbirds are on the wing now, and immature males are confusing: they resemble females — white-throated, plain — but often show a few scattered dark flecks or a single scarlet feather on the throat, the first hint of the gorget to come.
- Chokecherries ripen through their traffic-light sequence — green to red to glossy black-purple — with thicket birds tracking every stage; cedar waxwings, catbirds, and robins strip the reddest clusters, and what survives to full ripeness is astringent enough to pucker a stone but makes legendary syrup and jelly.
- This is typically the hottest stretch, though elevation shaves the worst edge off valley heat waves, and nights — draining cold air east down the slope — stay sleepable when Albany swelters.
- Bears shift into pre-**hyperphagia** feeding, working blackberries, raspberries, and any early mast; a bear will eat 20,000 or more berries in a day when a patch is good.
- Bucks' antlers reach full size and begin to mineralize under the velvet; the summer bachelor groups will break up within weeks.
- Thunderstorms build over the Helderberg edge on humid afternoons; the escarpment's *orographic lift* makes this hill country measurably stormier than the valley floor, and the light before a big cell crosses Still Hill rewards stepping outside.
- *Ghost:* **What stood here before.** A survey of this region in 1787 found only sixty-seven settlers, and a contemporary description recalled *"the terrific forests [of] overshadowing hemlock [that] had not then (1793) been felled"* and *"mountain sides . . . covered with dense thickets of somber-looking hemlocks."* That is the pre-clearance forest of these hills in the words of someone who saw it: not the mixed northern hardwoods of today but **hemlock**, dense and dark, on the slopes. Through the late 1700s the land was rapidly cleared for farming, and **sawmills and tanneries proliferated** — the tanneries running on hemlock bark. The forest was cut for its timber and stripped for its skin within a single generation, and what grew back was something else entirely.
- *Ghost:* Eastern hemlock is the tree that makes a summer ravine cold, and it has been hunted twice. In the nineteenth century it was felled across the Catskills and Helderbergs for its tannin-rich bark, stripped for the leather industry and the trunks frequently left where they fell to rot — an entire forest killed for its skin. It is being hunted again now by the hemlock woolly adelgid, working north as winters warm. And it was hunted once before that: pollen cores across eastern North America record a sudden hemlock collapse about 5,500 years ago, likely an insect or pathogen outbreak, from which the species needed a thousand years to recover. This is the third round of a very old fight.
- Peach harvest, in the years there is one, runs from late July into August depending on cultivar.
- *Health watch:* The early-color diagnostic opens now, and it is the most valuable free measurement available on this land. A maple showing red or orange in late July or early August is not being poetic — it is a stressed tree shutting down early, and it is the classic early symptom of maple decline. Healthy maples here color from about the third week of September. Anything coloring more than three weeks ahead of its neighbors is flagging itself. Note which individuals do it, and where they stand on the slope.
- **Garden:** fall lettuce and spinach seeds start in cool indoor conditions or deep shade (both germinate poorly in hot soil); last succession beets go in by month's end to beat the September frost.

### 15. Chokecherries Darken — August 1–15

- The chokecherry crop hits full ripeness and the harvest race is on — waxwing flocks can strip a laden shrub in an afternoon.
- Goldenrod opens its first sprays, the opening note of the hill's longest bloom season.
- Monarchs of the migratory generation begin emerging; unlike their parents these will live eight months and fly to Mexico, and every milkweed left standing is a contribution to that absurd, magnificent journey.
- The Perseid meteor shower peaks August 11–13 — the year's most watchable, fixed and reliable, radiating from the northeast after midnight into the hill's ideally aimed dark eastern horizon.
- Nights turn noticeably: the first genuinely cool overnight lows arrive, often startlingly so — the hilltop can touch the 40s in mid-August while the valley holds 60 — and the katydid tempo slows with the temperature (katydids are a functioning thermometer; their pulse rate tracks the degrees).
- Barn swallow and tree swallow flocks begin staging on wires in the valley.
- **Bear hyperphagia** begins in earnest — the annual feeding frenzy in which a bear may consume 15,000–20,000 calories a day and gain several pounds daily, foraging up to twenty hours out of twenty-four. Every bear conflict of late summer and autumn traces to this single physiological imperative, and this is when unsecured garbage, beehives, corn, and fruit trees become genuinely magnetic.
- Chipmunks begin filling the winter larder, working with *cheek pouches* that can hold a startling volume — a chipmunk carrying a full load looks visibly deformed and can transport dozens of seeds per trip.
- Hummingbirds enter **hyperphagia** — feeding compulsively to lay down fat for migration, often putting on 25–40% of body weight in a couple of weeks and becoming visibly rotund. Feeders should be kept full and clean through this window and well past it; there is no truth to the notion that a feeder delays departure, since migration is triggered by day length, not food supply.
- Adult males depart first, often by mid-to-late August, followed by females and then by juveniles — meaning the last hummingbirds at a feeder in September are usually young birds making the journey to Central America alone, with no adult to follow and no learned route. The navigation is entirely innate.
- The light itself changes — sun angle lowering, shadows lengthening, the unmistakable amber cast that means the year has crested.
- *Ghost:* American beech is currently mid-erasure — hit first by beech bark disease (a scale insect opening the way for a fungus, spreading since the early 1900s) and now by beech leaf disease, a nematode-associated affliction that arrived in Ohio in 2012 and reached New York within a few years. Beech was the other great mast tree beside the chestnut and oak, and beechnuts fed passenger pigeons, bears, and turkeys alike. Watching it go is the closest thing available to seeing the chestnut blight happen in real time.
- *Health watch:* Peak window for mapping premature fall color. Walking the same route in the first half of August each year and marking every early-coloring tree builds, over a few seasons, a decline map of the property — and the pattern usually clusters by site rather than by species, pointing at thin soil, ledge, exposure, or a drainage problem underneath.
- **Garden:** fall radishes, lettuce, and spinach direct-sow mid-month; oats-and-peas sow as a winterkill cover crop on any bed finishing early, building biomass that frost will lay down as mulch.

- **Foodways — green corn, and the year's second thanksgiving.** The first roasting ears are ready now, and the **Haudenosaunee Green Corn Ceremony** falls in this window — a multi-day observance marking the corn becoming edible, and one of the major ceremonies of the year. It is the moment the hungry gap of April is finally and completely over.
- **Chokecherries are ripening as the name of this microseason says**, and they were a staple across Indigenous North America — pounded whole, stones and all, and dried into cakes, or mixed with fat and dried meat. Astringent raw and transformed by cooking, they became jelly and syrup in every later kitchen on this hill. **The stones contain cyanogenic compounds and traditional preparation accounts for this**; cooked and strained products are the safe form.
- **Staghorn sumac drupes are red and ready** — steeped in cold water they make the tart pink drink often called Indian lemonade, used by Indigenous peoples here and adopted by settlers. Cold water only; hot water draws out the tannins and ruins it.

### 16. Goldenrod Tide — August 16–31

- Goldenrod floods the old field — multiple species in sequence — joined by the first asters, together running the hill's last great nectar economy: bumblebees, migrating monarchs, locust borers in yellow-and-black goldenrod camouflage, and the ambush bugs and crab spiders that hunt them all.
- Goldenrod, insect-pollinated with heavy sticky pollen, does not cause hay fever; that slander belongs to ragweed, blooming invisibly nearby on the wind.
- Hazelnuts ripen in their leafy husks (*involucres*), and the raids begin — blue jays and squirrels can empty a shrub days before the nuts read as ready; the traditional test is that when a husk twists off easily, the harvest is already late. Early picking and curing, or conscious surrender to the wildlife, are the two honorable options.
- Common nighthawks migrate through in loose, buoyant flocks on late-August evenings — one of the region's most underrated spectacles, best watched from an open hilltop.
- The two hoarding strategies now run side by side, and the ecological consequences differ completely. Gray squirrels and blue jays are **scatter hoarders** — thousands of single-item caches, many forgotten, which is how forests get planted. Chipmunks and red squirrels are **larder hoarders**, concentrating everything in one defended store, from which nothing germinates. A landscape's tree regeneration depends heavily on which strategy dominates.
- Gray squirrels also practice deceptive caching: when they believe they are being watched, they perform elaborate *fake* burials — digging, miming a deposit, covering an empty hole — while keeping the nut in the mouth for later. This is documented behavior, not folklore.
- *Zugunruhe* — the ornithological term for migratory restlessness — is now visible everywhere: birds flocking, feeding urgently, oriented south.
- **Blue jay.** The hazelnut raids are the opening of the most ecologically consequential behavior any bird on this property performs. A jay carries nuts in an expandable **gular pouch** in the throat plus one in the bill and one at the bill tip — typically five to seven acorns per trip — and caches them singly, pushed into soil or leaf litter, often far from the parent tree. Blue jays will carry seed as far as 2.5 miles, the longest documented seed-transport distance of any bird.
- The scale is extraordinary: an individual adult jay harvests and caches several thousand nuts in a single autumn, and one study recorded fifty jays moving 150,000 acorns in twenty-eight days. Jays also select viable nuts preferentially — the great majority of what they bury is sound seed, because they are choosing food, not planting trees.
- What they forget is a forest. Johnson and Webb's 1989 work in the *Journal of Biogeography* argued that jay caching explains how oaks, beeches, and chestnuts — heavy-seeded trees that cannot travel on wind — migrated north after the ice at rates matching light wind-dispersed species. In the deep-time terms of this calendar: the oaks followed the retreating Laurentide ice back up this valley in the throats of blue jays.
- *Ghost:* The idea that this landscape was unbroken forest before contact is a colonial invention. Haudenosaunee peoples and their predecessors kept openings deliberately and at scale — burning to maintain berry ground, nut orchards, browse for deer, and clear sightlines, and managing chestnut and hickory stands as something much closer to agriculture than to wilderness. An old field full of goldenrod and hazel is not a departure from the historical landscape. It is a crude, accidental echo of it.
- Virginia creeper turns first — well before anything else, sometimes in the third week of August, and it turns a pure hot scarlet that stands out against a wholly green woods. This is one early color change that means nothing bad: creeper is simply an early-senescing vine, and its precocity is normal, not stress. Knowing that keeps it from contaminating the early-color survey.
- *Health watch:* Spotted lanternfly adults are on the wing from late August through October, and this is the season to know the animal. At rest it is a drab gray-tan; when it jumps or flies it flashes scarlet underwings with black spots, unlike anything else here. It feeds on over seventy plants, with grape, maple, walnut, hops, and fruit trees among its favorites, and its preferred host is invasive tree-of-heaven. It is established in the Hudson Valley and around Buffalo, Binghamton, and Ithaca, with hitchhiker detections in Albany County going back to 2018. Sightings outside the established zones are worth reporting to iMapInvasives or NY Ag & Markets.
- **Garden:** last call for fall spinach sown outdoors; a late-August spinach sowing can also overwinter under mulch for the earliest spring greens.

---

- **Black gum turns first, and it turns scarlet.** While every other hardwood is still fully green, individual black gums begin flaring red in the last days of August — the earliest reliable colour on the hill, weeks ahead of the maples, and startling out of a green canopy. A single early-scarlet crown in August is almost always tupelo.
- **Its fruit ripens with the colour**: small blue-black drupes, very high in fat, dropping exactly as thrushes, catbirds and the first warblers move south. Fat-rich autumn fruit is migration fuel, and black gum is among the best providers of it here.

- **Cardinal flower comes into the wet ground now, and it is unmistakable** — a spike of intense scarlet, the strongest red on the property. **It is built specifically for hummingbirds**: bees see red poorly and cannot reach into the tube, so the flower is effectively reserved for birds, and it opens exactly when hummingbirds are packing on fat for migration.
- **Jewelweed fills the shaded wet ground** with hanging orange spotted lanterns, also hummingbird flowers, also nectar-rich, also blooming at the moment of maximum demand. **Its seed pods explode at a touch** — the "touch-me-not" name — coiling open and flinging seed several feet. The green seeds inside taste distinctly of walnut.
- **It is an annual**, which is unusual for something so dominant, and it reseeds itself into the same ground every year from those explosions.

## FALL

### 17. Hazel Harvest Moonrise — September 1–15

- Whatever hazelnuts the jays spared drop now; the full moon nearest this window rises huge and amber over the eastern valley — an east-facing aspect makes moonrise the hill's signature astronomical event, and the September full moon over the Basic Creek lowlands is the year's best.
- Broad-winged hawks begin streaming south, sometimes in kettles of dozens riding the escarpment's ridge lift; the Helderberg edge is a genuine, if minor, hawk-migration flyway.
- The last hummingbirds pass through, and the departure is worth marking: a specific date, written down each year, is one of the cleanest phenological measurements this hill offers, because the bird is unmistakable and the event is sharp.
- Leaving a feeder up through October and even November is worth doing, because western vagrants — **rufous hummingbird** most often, an orange bird from the Pacific Northwest that increasingly winters along the Gulf coast — turn up in the Northeast in late fall with real regularity. A hummingbird at a New York feeder in November is very likely not a ruby-throat.
- Jay caching begins in earnest, and the flight lines become obvious: birds crossing open ground repeatedly in the same direction with full throats, all day, for weeks.
- First cold nights arrive in earnest — the hilltop can flirt with frost in early September while the valley stays comfortably clear, and the cold hollows whiten first.
- Monarchs depart in singles and small strings, all bearing southwest; ruby-throated hummingbirds vanish by mid-month, feeders suddenly, definitively still.
- Gray dogwood foliage begins its turn toward wine-purple as its berries whiten; wood asters carry the shade edges; the goldenrod tide begins to brown at the crest.
- Crickets replace katydids as the night's tempo section, slowing audibly with each cooler evening.
- Bear activity peaks with the mast drop. Southern Black Bear Range has been expanding northward into southern Albany and Schoharie Counties in recent decades, and this hill sits squarely in that advancing front — bears here are a comparatively recent and still-increasing presence, not a fixed background condition. Cornell density work in southern New York estimated roughly one bear per three square miles.
- Bucks strip their velvet in the first half of September — a process of a day or less, leaving bloody shreds hanging and the antler stained dark by sap and bark before polishing to ivory tips.
- Bear sign to learn now: claw-marked and bark-scarred beech and cherry, whole limbs broken and pulled inward on wild apple and cherry, and the distinctive tangled mass of broken branches high in a tree that trackers call a **bear nest** — the wreckage left where a bear sat and fed.
- *Ghost:* The mast economy this fortnight once ran is nearly unimaginable now. Chestnut, beech, oak, hickory, and hazel dropping in concert fed passenger pigeons in the billions, black bears, turkeys, and the people who timed their year to it — a pulse of fat and starch arriving on schedule across an entire continent. Chestnut is gone, beech is going, and the oaks that carry the load now boom and bust with the late frosts the chestnut used to sidestep. The mast still falls. It falls thinner, and less reliably, and to a much smaller audience.
- Wild grapes ripen small, dark, and intensely sour-sweet, and the whole thicket community works them — catbirds, robins, foxes, raccoons, and black bears. Riverbank grape stays tart enough to make excellent jelly and indifferent eating.
- Virginia creeper berries ripen deep blue-black on coral-red stems, the same red-advertisement strategy the gray dogwood uses, and for the same reason: high-fat fruit sold to migrating birds. The berries are toxic to people — they contain oxalate crystals — and should be admired rather than sampled.
- Early apple varieties come in; old seedling trees are wildly variable and worth tasting individually, since roughly one wild apple in a hundred is genuinely good and the rest are cider.
- **Garden:** winter rye sows from now into early October as the workhorse overwintering cover crop — it germinates in cold soil, holds ground against erosion all winter, and springs back green in March.

- **Acorns begin to fall, and the white oak group falls first.** Chestnut, white and swamp white oak acorns drop from early September, are comparatively low in tannin, and **germinate immediately where they land** — a chestnut oak acorn on the ground in October will already have pushed a root into the leaf litter before winter. The red oak group holds later and does not germinate until spring.
- **That difference sets the whole autumn table.** Low-tannin white-group acorns are eaten first and preferentially by deer, turkey, bear, jay and squirrel; the bitter red-group acorns keep better and become the late-winter reserve. A hill with both groups feeds animals across a longer season than a hill with either alone.
- **Mast years are synchronous and consequential.** Oaks across a whole region produce heavily in the same year, every two to five years, then produce almost nothing — a strategy that starves seed predators in lean years and overwhelms them in heavy ones. **A heavy mast year is followed by a bear-heavy autumn, a mouse-heavy winter, and, two years later, a tick-heavy summer**, because white-footed mouse populations track acorns and blacklegged ticks track mice.
- **Shagbark hickory nuts drop through September**, husks splitting cleanly into four sections and releasing a nut that is genuinely excellent eating — sweeter than pecan, and the reason the tree was left standing in so many old pastures.
- **Blue jays are the oak's dispersal system.** A single jay can cache thousands of acorns in an autumn, carrying several at a time in throat and bill and burying them singly at the right depth in open ground. The ones it forgets become trees. Oak migration northward after the glaciers moved faster than gravity alone can explain, and jays are the leading explanation.

### 18. Dogwood Berries White — September 16–30

- Gray dogwood berries ripen — porcelain-white drupes on branching pedicels of shocking coral-red, one of the most striking color pairings in the native flora — and migrant birds arrive to strip them precisely on schedule.
- The timing is no accident: dogwood fruit is unusually lipid-rich, premium migration fuel advertised by those red stems; thrushes, catbirds, vireos, and warblers working the thickets now will winter in Central America.
- The autumnal equinox passes (September 21–23).
- First frost typically strikes the hill in this window — a week or more ahead of the valley — often as a patchwork revealing the microclimate in white: cold hollows and the Still Hill shadow line first, the warm east brow last; the pattern is the property's honest thermal portrait.
- Sugar maples begin turning from the top down and the outside in.
- Blue jay migration runs now along ridgelines and lake shores, and it is one of the genuine open questions in North American ornithology. Some jays migrate and some do not; the same individual may go south one year, stay the next, and go again the year after. No one has worked out what governs it. Loose flocks of dozens moving purposefully south are migrants; the jays at the feeder may be residents, or may be replacements from farther north.
- Turkeys shift to autumn flocking and sex-segregate: hens with this year's poults in large mixed groups, toms in separate bachelor bands. Scratched-over patches in the leaf litter — fresh soil turned in irregular arcs — are the most reliable turkey sign of the season.
- White-throated sparrows return from the north, the *Old Sam Peabody* whistle suddenly back in the thickets after a five-month absence — the true sound of fall arriving on this hill.
- Chipmunks are in maximum larder mode and will abruptly vanish from view in October — not migrated and not dead, simply underground with the door plugged.
- Flying squirrels cache nuts in cavities and bark crevices, and their autumn activity is the best chance of the year to detect them: a soft, birdlike *chuck* from the dark canopy, and the faint scrabbling of something landing on bark after a glide.
- *Ghost:* White ash turns first and turns a color nothing else makes — a smoky purple-bronze that shows up in the third week of September. It is also the tree currently being deleted. The emerald ash borer reached New York in 2009 and has since worked through Albany County and beyond, and ash mortality approaches total wherever it establishes. That purple is worth stopping for, because within a generation it will be a color this landscape no longer produces in autumn.
- *Health watch:* Ash decline is legible now on three signs at once. Crown thinning starts at the top and works down. *Blonding* appears on the trunk — pale patches where woodpeckers have flaked away the outer bark hunting larvae, visible from a distance and often the very first symptom anyone notices. And *epicormic sprouts*, tufts of desperate new growth erupting straight from the trunk and larger limbs, mean the tree is trying to rebuild a canopy it can no longer support. Under the bark, the borer's galleries are flat S-shaped tunnels packed with frass, and the adult exit holes are a distinctive D-shape about an eighth of an inch across.
- **Garden:** tender crops finish at first frost — squash and pumpkins harvest before any hard freeze, cured warm for storage; green tomatoes ripen indoors; winter rye sowing continues on cleared beds.

- **Foodways — apples, and everything made from them.** The old orchard trees come in now, and almost none of it was eaten fresh. **Cider was the default drink of the rural Northeast**, safer than water and made by everyone; **cider vinegar** followed as the year's preserving acid. **Schnitz** — apples quartered, cored and dried on racks or strung on strings above the stove — is the Palatine German answer to a glut, and schnitz went into pies, into soups, and into winter with pork.
- **Apple butter is a day-long communal operation**, a copper kettle over a fire and constant stirring from morning to night, the whole point being to reduce a bushel of fruit to something that keeps without sugar. **It is a social event as much as a preserving one**, and it belongs to this fortnight.
- **Nuts come down in the same weeks.** Hickory, butternut and walnut were gathered heavily by Indigenous peoples here — **hickory milk**, made by pounding nuts shell and all, boiling, and skimming the rich emulsion, was a significant food rather than a curiosity. Butternut also yielded a dye that gave "butternut" its second meaning as a colour.

### 19. Maple Blaze on the East Slope — October 1–15

- Peak foliage — reliably about a week ahead of the Hudson Valley, typically landing in the first ten days of October: old-field maples burn orange-scarlet, chokecherries go yellow-bronze, and black locust skips autumn entirely, dropping its leaflets yellow-green, done.
- The east-facing slope catches sunrise light through the peak canopy; the first hour after dawn is the hill's absolute annual best.
- Hard frost becomes routine.
- Sparrow migration peaks — song, swamp, white-throated, white-crowned, with dark-eyed juncos arriving to stay — and the thickets seethe with them; ruby-crowned and golden-crowned kinglets pass through.
- Peak jay caching, and peak jay noise. The screaming *jay-jay* is only part of the repertoire: blue jays are accomplished mimics of red-shouldered and red-tailed hawks, and a convincing hawk scream from a feeder area often clears it of every other bird for a few useful seconds. Whether this is deliberate deception or an alarm honestly given remains debated; the effect is the same.
- Northern saw-whet owl migration peaks in October, moving at night, and the banding network that revealed it — nets and audiolures run across the Northeast — turned a bird once considered rare into one now known to be common and simply invisible. They pass over this hill in numbers on cold clear October nights.
- Chickadees are caching hard now, storing thousands of seeds one item per site, and their brains change to handle it: the **hippocampus enlarges seasonally**, with new neurons added in autumn, then shrinks again — an animal growing extra memory hardware for the season in which it needs it, and one of the founding examples of adult neurogenesis in vertebrates.
- The last woolly bear caterpillars cross lanes toward hibernation (their bands predict nothing, but counting them is tradition).
- Peak gray squirrel caching, and peak road mortality — dispersal, mast-chasing, and pre-winter frenzy combine. In heavy mast failure years, gray squirrels historically undertook mass emigrations, and nineteenth-century accounts from New York describe squirrels crossing rivers and roads in numbers that stopped traffic.
- Deer enter the pre-rut: bucks make **rubs** (shredding sapling bark with the antler base, depositing forehead-gland scent) and **scrapes** (pawed bare earth beneath an overhanging *licking branch*, which is the actual communication device — the ground is almost incidental).
- Buck sign appears: rubs on saplings and tree tubes, and the first scrapes scraped bare beneath licking branches.
- The Orionid meteors (dust of Halley's Comet) peak around October 21–22, radiating from Orion as he returns to the late-evening sky — winter's advance scout.
- *Ghost:* The foliage is genuinely diminished, and not subtly. A pre-1900 October on this escarpment included the chestnut's clear gold and the elm's yellow arching over every lane, on trees several times the size of anything standing now, in a forest whose canopy dominants have been swapped out twice over. What remains is spectacular. It is also a partial cast performing a play written for a much larger company.
- Main apple harvest — later here than in the valley, and the late-hanging seedling apples improve markedly after the first frosts, which convert starch to sugar and take the hard edge off.
- *Health watch:* Fallen fruit and leaves are inoculum. Apple scab and black rot both overwinter in litter beneath the tree, so raking out and removing dropped leaves and mummified fruit from any apple worth keeping breaks the cycle more effectively than anything applied from a sprayer.
- **Garden:** garlic planting month — cloves go in mid-to-late October, 2–4 weeks before the ground freezes, rooting in the cold and mulched heavily before winter; the hill's early freeze argues for the early side of the window.

- **Shagbark hickory is the gold in the October woods.** Where maple supplies scarlet and orange, hickory turns a clear deep yellow that holds for a week or more, and on a grey day it reads as the brightest thing on the hillside.
- **The oaks come last and hold longest**, running to russet, bronze, deep wine and leathery brown after the maples have dropped. Chestnut oak turns a dull yellow-brown; red oak and black oak go to a dark red-brown. **An oak-dominated slope stays coloured a full two to three weeks after a maple slope has gone bare** — which is the practical argument for planting oaks as maple succession, quite apart from soil.
- **Hop hornbeam turns a soft clear yellow** in the understory, easy to miss beneath the canopy show above it.

### 20. Frost Flowers at First Hard Freeze — October 16–31

- The hill's rarest and most fragile spectacle becomes possible: on the first hard freezes — clear, calm nights in the low-to-mid 20s while soil is still unfrozen and wet — sap drawn up certain dead herbaceous stems extrudes through split stems as ribbons of ice, curling into white rosettes: frost flowers. They form in darkness and die at first sun; the search window is dawn along the field edges, and finding even one is a good year.
- The same damp-cold conditions on rotting hardwood — dead locust and maple limbs, fungal-ridden — can produce *hair ice*: silken white filaments curled like combed wool, grown overnight by the fungus *Exidiopsis effusa*.
- Leaf-fall completes; the woods reopen and the land's true architecture shows again — stone rows, the slope's grain, the deer highways.
- Foxfire becomes findable: on damp, mild, moonless nights, honey-mushroom mycelium in rotting wood glows faint green — full dark-adaptation (twenty minutes, no phone) required.
- Bears den progressively from late October through December, pregnant females first and large males last — and in a heavy mast year some southern-zone bears delay denning well into winter or barely den at all.
- Porcupines return to winter dens in rock crevices and hollow trees, often the same den across generations, and the scat pile at the entrance is the marker.
- The rut builds toward its November peak; bucks move at all hours.
- Great horned owls resume vocal territorial dueling now, three months ahead of nesting — October and November evenings are the best hooting of the year.
- Turkeys concentrate on mast, and the flocks grow. In a poor acorn year they range widely and turn up in unexpected places; in a heavy mast year they can stay within a few hundred acres all winter.
- Last hard invasive-cutting window of the year: honeysuckle holds green leaves weeks after the natives drop — highlighted again, now in reverse.
- *Ghost:* Timber rattlesnakes return to their ancestral dens in October, and the limestone ledges and talus of the Helderberg escarpment are classic denning country — dens used continuously for centuries, sometimes millennia, by snakes that return to the exact crevice where they were born. New York paid bounties on them into the 1970s, and organized den-raiding wiped out most colonies. The species is now threatened in the state and gone from most of its former range here. The dens are still in the rock. Many of them are simply empty.
- *Health watch:* Spotted lanternfly egg masses go on from now through winter and are findable until May — inch-long grayish smears that look like dried mud or a swipe of putty, laid on trunks, rocks, fence posts, equipment, and vehicles indiscriminately. Anything that has traveled from downstate is worth a look before it is parked for the winter.
- **Garden:** garlic planting concludes; a final rye sowing can still catch on mild years; poppy seed can alternatively be broadcast now for natural winter stratification.

- **Northern bayberry holds its leaves while everything else drops them.** Semi-evergreen, aromatic when crushed, it keeps a dull olive foliage into winter and is one of the few shrubs that reads as green on the hill in November.
- **The berries are grey-blue and coated in true wax.** Boiled, they release it — roughly a pound of berries to an ounce of wax — and **bayberry candles were a real colonial industry**, valued because they burn cleanly and smell of the plant rather than of tallow. Gathering is a late-autumn job, after frost and before the birds finish them.
- **Bayberry fixes its own nitrogen**, but not in the way legumes do: it partners with *Frankia*, a filamentous bacterium, in an **actinorhizal** symbiosis. It is therefore a soil-builder on ground too poor for clover, which is precisely why it belongs on a thin windswept edge.
- Like red cedar it is **dioecious** — berries only on female plants, and only if a male is within reach.

- **Foodways — kraut, and the cabbage that carried the Germans through winter.** Cabbage is cut after the first frosts, which sweeten it, then shredded, salted and packed in crocks to ferment. **Sauerkraut is the central Palatine German winter vegetable**, and it survives because lactic fermentation needs no fuel, no sugar and no sealing — only salt, and a cool place. A crock started now works slowly all winter in a cold cellar.
- **Potatoes are dug in this window too, and the Irish shadow falls across them.** The Famine of 1845–52 pushed enormous numbers of Irish into New York, and the ones who reached the Hilltowns arrived as farm labour and quarry hands. **Potatoes had been a New World crop taken to Ireland and brought back again**, and on a thin acid upland like this they grow better than wheat ever did.
- **Samhain falls at the end of this microseason** — the Gaelic quarter-day marking summer's end and the boundary of the dark half of the year, and the origin of Hallowe'en. **It is a harvest deadline as much as a festival**: everything still in the field was considered finished after it, and what remained belonged to the spirits or the birds.

- **Sensitive fern earns its name in a single night.** It is among the first plants on the hill to react to a hard freeze, **going black and collapsing within hours** while ferns around it are still green. **It is a usable first-frost marker** — more precise than a thermometer reading taken at the house, because it records what happened at ground level in the low wet ground where frost settles first.
- **Spicebush fruit is scarlet and extremely high in fat**, ripening exactly as thrushes and catbirds move south. Fat-rich autumn fruit is migration fuel, and spicebush is among the best sources of it here. **Only the female plants carry it** — the species is dioecious, so a fruitless spicebush is not failing, it is male.

### 21. Hair Ice on the Fallen Locust — November 1–15

- Peak conditions for hair ice as the freeze-thaw pendulum swings daily.
- Peak white-tailed deer rut — the hill's largest common mammals at their most heedless, chasing through dogwood thickets at midday; rutting bucks spar with unstaked tree tubes, making this the season for a pre-winter staking check.
- The rut is run on chemistry as much as antlers. Bucks rub-urinate over the **tarsal glands** on the inside of the hocks, where bacteria convert the compounds into the dark, pungent staining visible on any rutting buck, broadcasting individual identity and status. Interdigital, preorbital, and forehead glands add layers to the same signal system.
- Peak breeding here falls in a tight window in mid-November, timed so that fawns drop in late May and early June — which means the rut date is really a *fawn-drop* date working backward through a 200-day gestation, set by the northern climate rather than the deer.
- Deer are in full winter coat now: hollow guard hairs that trap air so effectively that snow accumulates on a bedded deer's back without melting. A deer wearing unmelted snow is a well-insulated deer, not a cold one.
- Dark-eyed juncos are fully settled, flashing white outer tail feathers along every edge, and the winter feeder guild assembles.
- The last robin and blackbird flocks pass through, stripping what fruit remains.
- Crow roosts reassemble to full winter size, and the pre-roost staging behavior is worth watching at dusk: birds gather in intermediate assembly trees, mill and call for twenty or thirty minutes, then move to the roost proper in a single mass departure.
- Red-winged blackbirds have gone entirely, joining enormous mixed winter roosts with grackles, cowbirds, and starlings far to the south — aggregations that can number in the millions and represent one of the largest concentrations of birds anywhere on the continent.
- Tamaracks in the valley wetlands — the region's one deciduous conifer — turn smoky gold, the season's final blaze, visible from eastern viewpoints.
- The Leonid meteors peak November 16–18 — modest most years, but the shower with a history of legendary storms.
- Orion clears the eastern horizon by mid-evening; the winter sky assembles in order — Aldebaran, then Orion, then Sirius — like stagehands setting the next act.
- Stick season's austere palette settles in: oxblood dogwood stems, bleached goldenrod, gray locust bark, and the year's longest sightlines.
- *Ghost:* Beavers are cutting and caching now, and every one of them descends from a near-miss. The North American fur trade ran on this animal, and the Dutch and English traded for beaver pelts out of Albany for two centuries; by 1900 New York State held perhaps a few dozen wild beavers total. They were restocked, they took, and they are now everywhere — one of conservation's genuine, unqualified successes. The wetlands they build along Basic Creek are not a restoration of something old. They are the old thing itself, switched back on.
- **Fruit tree winter prep**, and the deadline is the first lasting snow. Voles girdle young apple and peach trunks under the snowpack, chewing a complete ring of bark at or just below the snow line where nothing can see them, and a girdled tree is dead in spring with no warning at all — quarter-inch hardware cloth cylinders, sunk an inch into the soil and standing well above expected snow depth, are the only reliable answer. Light-colored trunk wrap or white latex on the southwest side of young trunks prevents *sunscald*, the winter injury caused when low February sun warms bark on a cold day and the tissue is killed by the refreeze after sundown.

- **Tamarack is the last colour of the year, and it is the only conifer that does this.** A deciduous larch, it turns pure gold in late October and holds into November after every hardwood has finished, then drops its needles entirely and stands bare and grey all winter — a conifer that looks dead for five months every year and is not. In wet ground it is one of the few trees that thrives on saturated acid soil.
- **The oaks begin marcescence now**, young trees keeping their dead russet leaves while older ones let go. The held leaves rattle all winter and will not fall until the buds push them off in April.
- **Blue jays are still caching acorns into November**, and the sound of the hill in this microseason is largely jays arguing over the last of the mast.

### 22. The Gray Time — November 16–30

- The most subdued fortnight, and quietly among the most useful: the land is fully legible — every contour, drainage, deer trail, and stone row exposed — making this the natural season for an annual walking audit: mapping honeysuckle and multiflora for the next campaign, noting which hazels have outgrown their tubes, reading the summer's erosion in bare soil.
- Regular firearms deer season opens in the Southern Zone around November 21 — blaze orange for every walk; expect deer to shift nocturnal and secretive within days of opening morning, as they know the calendar as well as anyone.
- First sticking snows typically arrive on the hilltop in this window, whitening the heights while the valley stays brown — the microclimate's winter debut.
- Flocks of snow buntings may swirl over open fields — arctic visitors that treat a Helderberg hilltop as a reasonable substitute for tundra.
- Turkeys are at their most visible against snow, and their winter strategy is conservative: they roost in trees at night, feed in short bouts, and in deep powder may sit in the roost for several days at a stretch rather than burn energy floundering. Crusted snow is far worse for them than deep soft snow, because they cannot scratch through it to reach mast.
- Ravens follow the deer season, scavenging gut piles alongside crows and coyotes — one of the more reliable ways to hear ravens on this hill in a given year, and a good demonstration of how quickly they locate carrion.
- The last hardy fungi — late oysters on dead locust — persist through the freezes.
- A full mammal roster for a property of this size and structure, worth working through deliberately: white-tailed deer, black bear, coyote, red fox, gray fox (more arboreal and far less often seen — the only American canid that routinely climbs trees), bobcat, fisher, ermine, long-tailed weasel, mink, river otter and beaver and muskrat along Basic Creek, raccoon, striped skunk, Virginia opossum, woodchuck, porcupine, eastern cottontail, snowshoe hare at higher elevations, gray squirrel, red squirrel, southern flying squirrel, eastern chipmunk, meadow vole, southern red-backed vole, white-footed and deer mice, meadow jumping mouse, star-nosed and hairy-tailed moles, and several shrews. Absent and once present: wolf, cougar, elk, lynx, marten, wolverine.
- Gray fox is the most consistently overlooked: smaller-footed than a red fox, grizzled with a black-tipped tail (a red fox's tail tip is white), and largely nocturnal. Its numbers have fallen in recent decades where coyotes are dense, since coyotes kill foxes on sight.
- By month's end the hill is down to about nine hours of daylight, and Still Hill's lengthening afternoon shadow arrives noticeably earlier each week — the local mountain taxing the sun at its steepest seasonal rate.
- *Ghost:* This is the fortnight for reading stone. A stone wall running through mature woods is a boundary from when those woods were a field, and its stones were dragged from that field by hand and by ox after frost pushed them up each spring. The wall is a fossil of labor. The stones themselves are fossils of something far older: Helderberg limestone is crowded with brachiopod shells, crinoid stem segments like tiny stacked poker chips, and horn corals — the accumulated skeletons of a shallow tropical sea from four hundred million years ago, hauled uphill by a farmer who wanted a straight line. Low winter sun raking across a wall face is the best light of the year for finding them. Look also for barbed wire swallowed into tree trunks — the tree grew around the fence, and the fence dates the abandonment.
- *Health watch:* The annual crown audit belongs here, when every canopy is bare and legible at once. Grapevine release also belongs here — cutting a heavy vine at the base with a second cut a foot higher (removing a section so the ends cannot rejoin) frees a loaded crown before winter ice arrives, and the severed top simply dies in place rather than needing to be pulled down. Look, too, for *conks*: shelf fungi emerging from a trunk or root flare are fruiting bodies of internal decay, which means the rot is years advanced and structurally significant by the time anything shows on the outside.

---

- **The yellow-rumped warbler is the reason bayberry matters here**, and it is one of the best facts in eastern ornithology: it is **the only warbler that can digest the wax coating on bayberry and wax myrtle fruit**, having gut enzymes and bile salts the others lack. That single ability lets it winter hundreds of miles further north than any other warbler in North America. A warbler on this hill in late November is almost certainly this one, and it is eating something no other warbler can.
- **The evergreens become the only structure left.** With the oaks finally bare and the tamarack needles down, red cedar, white spruce and pitch pine are the entire vertical green of the hill, and their value as shelter is suddenly obvious — for birds roosting out of wind, for deer bedding, and for anyone standing on the north side of them.

- **Foodways — hog killing, and why it happens exactly now.** This is the most weather-dependent food event of the rural year. **Butchering waits for cold reliable enough to chill a carcass overnight but before the ground freezes hard**, which on this hill means the back half of November into early December. Every culture that farmed here did it in the same window, because the thermometer decided, not the calendar.
- **Nothing was discarded.** Hams and shoulders to the smokehouse or the salt barrel; sides to bacon; **sausage made the same day** because it cannot wait; lard rendered and stored in crocks; the scraps and offal cooked with cornmeal or buckwheat into **scrapple**, which is the Palatine German *panhas* under an English name; the head to headcheese; the feet pickled. **Scrapple exists because a hog killed in November has to be eaten before it spoils and there is no refrigeration.**
- **It was communal and reciprocal.** Neighbours came, worked, and were paid in meat — and the obligation ran the other way when their turn came. **A hill farm's winter protein depended on the timing of a cold snap and on who owed whom.**

## WINTER (RETURN)

### 23. Snow Rollers on the Open Field — December 1–15

- Early snowpack season opens the window for the strangest rare phenomenon: snow rollers — natural snowballs the wind rolls across open ground, hollow-cored like jelly rolls, requiring a near-impossible recipe (a slick base layer, an inch of wet loose snow, temperatures near freezing, and wind strong enough to roll but not destroy). An open, breezy hilltop field is exactly the habitat; most people never see one, so every marginal-temperature snowfall with wind deserves a look.
- Tracking season opens in earnest with each fresh snow: fisher looping through the woodlot, red fox trotting the field edge in a dead-straight registered line, the sudden wing-swept crater where an owl connected.
- Track reading rewards learning the **gaits** rather than just the prints, since gait survives poor snow conditions that erase toe detail. Canines and felines walk in a nearly straight *direct-register* line, hind foot landing in the fore print — and the fox's line is straighter and neater than the coyote's. Weasels and fishers bound in paired 2–2 tracks. Rabbits and squirrels land in a 4-print cluster with the large hind feet *ahead* of the front, so the tracks appear to point backward. Bobcat shows no claw marks and a distinctly asymmetrical, leading-toe pad; a dog or coyote shows claws.
- The cleanest fox-versus-coyote discriminator in soft snow is not size but the fox's **hair-covered foot pads**, which blur the print into an indistinct oval where a coyote's registers crisply.
- Snow is the hill's daily newspaper; morning is the edition worth reading.
- The Geminid meteors peak December 13–14 — the year's most reliable shower, rich and slow, and unusually sourced from an asteroid (3200 Phaethon) rather than a comet; cold and moonlight permitting, it outperforms the Perseids.
- Golden-crowned kinglets — improbable quarter-ounce survivors — work the conifers through weather that should kill them; how they manage (shivering, huddling, constant feeding) is a season's meditation in itself.
- The chickadee's role in the winter flock guild is informational: nuthatches, titmice, kinglets, creepers, and downy woodpeckers all **eavesdrop on chickadee alarm calls**, and several respond appropriately to the *dee*-note threat grading despite not producing it themselves. The chickadee is the flock's alarm system, and the other species are subscribers.
- Irruption years bring northern owls south when the boreal vole and lemming cycle crashes — **snowy owls** on open hilltops and airfields, more rarely northern shrikes hunting from exposed perches. An open Helderberg hilltop is exactly the habitat a snowy owl mistakes for tundra, and mid-December through February is the window.
- *Ghost:* Thirteen thousand years ago this fortnight's conditions were simply the climate, and the tracks in the snow would have been caribou. Paleoindian hunters worked exactly this kind of high open ground — the West Athens Hill site, a few ridges south along the same escarpment, was a hilltop chert quarry and workshop where people came to make tools and, from the elevation, to watch. The impulse to climb a hill in December and read what has crossed the snow is not a hobby. It is the oldest continuous human activity this landscape has hosted.

- **A windbreak is doing its work now, and the snow shows exactly how well.** Wind carrying snow slows as it meets a belt of trees and drops its load on the lee side; the drift that builds there is a direct readout of where the shelter falls. **Protection extends roughly ten times the height of the trees downwind**, strongest between two and five times — so a thirty-foot belt quiets about three hundred feet behind it.
- **A porous windbreak outperforms a solid one**, which is counterintuitive and worth knowing: air forced entirely over a dense wall tumbles down behind it in turbulent eddies, while a belt of roughly half-open structure lets enough through to keep the flow smooth and the calm deeper. Mixed, layered, slightly ragged plantings work better than tight hedges.
- **Red cedar earns its place in this month.** It holds branches to the ground for decades where spruce eventually shades its own lower limbs off, and dense low foliage is the entire point of a winter windbreak — wind cuts underneath a belt that has lifted its skirts.

- **Winterberry is the reason this month has colour.** A deciduous holly, it drops every leaf and stands as bare grey stems **hung with dense scarlet fruit**, the most vivid thing on the hill from November into February. Like spicebush and red cedar it is **dioecious** — berries only on females, and only with a male in range.
- **The birds leave it until late, and that is deliberate on both sides.** The fruit is low in fat and high in tannin, unpalatable while better food exists, and it needs repeated freeze-and-thaw cycles to soften and sweeten. **It is a late-winter reserve** — robins, bluebirds and waxwings strip it in February and March, when almost nothing else is left, and the plant gets its seeds dispersed at the season when there is least competition for the service.

### 24. Long Night on Albany Hill — December 16–31

- The winter solstice (December 21–22): the year's shortest day — roughly nine hours of light, minus whatever Still Hill's shadow claims from the afternoon — and its longest night, over fifteen hours of dark.
- The sun rises and sets at its southern extremes; the solstice sunrise point on the eastern horizon closes the calendar's loop.
- The Ursid meteors peak quietly on solstice night itself — a low-rate shower for purists, radiating from the Little Dipper.
- Barred owls call through the long dark — *who-cooks-for-you* volleys echoing between woodlots — and on the stillest, coldest nights the whole sequence of deep-winter optics returns: light pillars over the valley, moon halos, moondogs, the occasional cryoseism cracking the frozen ground like a starting pistol for the new year.
- The Pleiades climb again toward their midwinter height, carrying the Haudenosaunee ceremonial year toward its Midwinter renewal.
- Great horned owl courtship duets peak in the last two weeks of December, running toward the February eggs — the year's first breeding behavior begins before the year's calendar has ended.
- Christmas Bird Count season runs mid-December into early January, the longest-running community science dataset in existence, begun in 1900. Local counts are always short of participants, and a single hilltop property at 1,200 feet contributes exactly the kind of upland coverage most counts lack.
- Hazels stand with catkins already formed and waiting; dogwood stems burn red against the snow; the sap is eight weeks from stirring. The hill completes its twenty-fourth season and begins, without pausing, its first.
- Bucks begin casting antlers from late December onward as testosterone falls, and the timing is individual — a stressed or heavily rutted buck drops earliest. A buck seen in January with one antler is a normal buck mid-shed, not an injured one.
- Bear cubs are being born in dens right now, somewhere within a few miles, to mothers who will not wake for the event.
- *Ghost:* The final and largest one. Twenty thousand years ago there was no hill here to stand on — or rather there was, buried under something on the order of a mile of ice, its summit a thousand feet below the surface of a glacier that stretched unbroken to Labrador. The rock underfoot was pressed down hundreds of feet by the weight and is still, measurably, rebounding. On the longest night of the year, in the coldest quarter of the calendar, it is worth holding both facts at once: that this landscape is an interglacial parenthesis, and that the ice is not a thing that happened to it but the ordinary condition it is currently, temporarily, on vacation from.
