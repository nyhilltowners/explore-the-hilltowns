## HG

### What it is
Mercury is a metal that is liquid at room temperature and evaporates easily, which is why it travels. It occurs naturally in rock at very low levels and is released by volcanoes and forest fires; burning coal and heating limestone in cement kilns release far more, and atmospheric mercury over the continental United States is now three to four times what it was before industry ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/mercury-contamination-aquatic-environments)). In water and wet soil, bacteria convert it to methylmercury, the form that builds up in living things and damages the developing nervous system. Three numbers govern it: 2 µg/L in drinking water ([EPA MCL](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)), 0.3 mg/kg of methylmercury in fish tissue ([EPA criterion](https://www.epa.gov/wqc/human-health-criteria-methylmercury)), and the state's meal-frequency fish advisories ([NYSDOH, Albany County](https://health.ny.gov/environmental/outdoors/fish/health_advisories/maps/docs/albany_co_final.pdf)).

### How it gets here
*From the air.* Almost all of the mercury in our lakes arrives from the sky. Kilns and boilers send it up as vapor; some falls within a few miles, the rest joins a global pool and comes down in rain and dust anywhere. Modeled deposition on the plateau is about 10 µg per square metre per year (dry, 8 km from Rensselaerville). The nearest large source is the Ravena cement plant, 17 miles east, which reported about 180 kg a year to the air in the mid-2000s and about 6 kg in 2024.

*Into fish.* Once in a lake it concentrates: methylmercury in predatory fish is typically more than a million times the level in the water they swim in ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/mercury-contamination-aquatic-environments)). Shallow, warm, oxygen-poor water with plenty of dead plant matter — Basic Creek Reservoir in August — is where bacteria make methylmercury fastest.

*Not from the ground.* Every groundwater and creek-water test on record is a non-detect. The rock here is not a mercury source. The tap-water reports pulled (Rensselaerville, Greenville, Cairo, Catskill, Albany) do not list mercury, which under the reporting rules means it was not detected.

### What it does
*People.* Methylmercury goes to the nervous system. Adults who ate heavily contaminated fish showed "tremors, incoordination, impaired vision, impaired learning and memory, and mood changes," and children exposed before birth had "learning, sensory, and movement problems" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=113&toxid=24)). The unborn child is the most sensitive, which is why New York's fish advisories set stricter limits for anyone who could become pregnant and for children under fifteen. Fish is the route; water at the levels found here is not. Mercury vapor (a broken thermometer, a kiln stack) attacks the kidneys and lungs instead, but no one on the plateau is exposed that way.

*Plants and animals.* Plants take up little mercury from soil; the problem is aquatic. Fish-eating birds are the sentinel: common loons carrying 3 µg/g or more of mercury in their blood raise 41% fewer chicks than loons below 1 µg/g, spend less time on the nest and less time foraging ([Evers et al. 2008](https://www.savetheboundarywaters.org/sites/default/files/attachments/54-evers-2008-adverse-effects-from-environmental-mercury-loads-on-breeding-common-loons_0.pdf)). Songbirds that eat spiders and insects from wetland edges carry mercury too. The local bird-blood samples (Hudson islands, 2005–2015) run 0.03–0.57 ppm — below the loon threshold, but from different species. Otters, mink and herons at the top of the lake food web carry the most; none has been sampled here.

### What is not known
Fish from Basic Creek Reservoir and Alcove have not been tested since 2000; the reservoir has bloomed every year since and blooms make methylmercury. No mercury measurement exists for any private well on the plateau. TRI reporting starts at 100 lb a year of mercury handled, so small boilers and crematoria are absent.

## PFOS

### What it is
PFOS (perfluorooctane sulfonic acid) is one of the PFAS family, man-made chains of carbon and fluorine that shed water, oil and heat. It does not occur in nature at all; every molecule on Earth was manufactured. The carbon–fluorine bond does not break down in soil or water, so it moves with groundwater essentially forever, and it stays in a human body for years — PFOS has an elimination half-life of 3 to 27 years, PFHxS 5 to 35 ([ATSDR toxicological profile](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxprofiles/tp200.pdf)). Two numbers govern it in tap water: New York's limit of 10 ng/L, in force since 2020, and EPA's 4 ng/L, set in April 2024 and kept in May 2025, with compliance due in 2029 or, under a 2026 proposal, 2031 ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas)). A nanogram per litre is a part per trillion.

### Who made it, and who still does
3M began making PFOS in the late 1940s and sold it for half a century as Scotchgard, firefighting foam and a hundred industrial uses; DuPont made PFOA to manufacture Teflon. 3M stopped PFOS production in 2002 after its own blood studies reached EPA, and PFOA was phased out of U.S. manufacture by 2015 under an EPA stewardship agreement ([EPA, GenX advisory](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2022-06/drinking-water-genx-2022.pdf)). The Stockholm Convention has since listed PFOS (2009), PFOA (2019) and PFHxS (2022) for global elimination with exemptions, but production continued in China and elsewhere, and the stockpiles — foam in fire-station lockers, treated carpet, landfill leachate, sludge on fields — keep leaching. The industry's answer was to shorten the chain: Chemours, spun out of DuPont in 2015, makes GenX (HFPO-DA) at Fayetteville, North Carolina as the PFOA replacement; PFBS and PFBA replaced PFOS in foams and coatings. Shorter chains leave the body faster (PFBS in about a month) but are "even more mobile in the environment than long-chain PFAS," slip through activated-carbon filters that catch the long chains, and are "associated with adverse health effects similar to those of the legacy PFAS they are replacing" ([NRDC](https://www.nrdc.org/bio/anna-reade/pfas-no-forever-exemptions-forever-chemicals)). EPA set a 10 ng/L limit on GenX and PFHxS in 2024 and proposed rescinding both in May 2026; PFBS, PFBA and the ultra-short chains now turning up in rain have no drinking-water limit at all. A well that reads clean for PFOS is not a well that has been checked for its replacements.

### How it gets here
*Foam and fabric, then groundwater.* The classic source is firefighting foam used at airports, military bases and fire-training grounds; the plume then rides groundwater downhill for decades. At Cannon Air Force Base in New Mexico, groundwater beside the training area holds 27,819 ng/L and perimeter wells 2 km away are clean; the shorter-chain PFHxS travels ahead of PFOS, so a well far from the source reads PFHxS-heavy. That order is the fingerprint used below.

*Septage and sludge on fields.* Biosolids and septage carry whatever went down the drain, PFAS included. In New York, septage cannot be spread on any field without a DEC registration (up to two trucks, at least 15 acres per truck, no more than 25,000 gallons per acre per year) or a permit above that, while manure from a farm's own animals needs no filing at all ([6 NYCRR 361-2.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/6-NYCRR-361-2.2), [361-2.3](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/6-NYCRR-361-2.3)). The registrant is the operator who spreads it, usually the hauler, so DEC's public list carries the hauler's business address, not the fields: Becker & Son's Septic Service in Berne, 11 km away, holds a land-application registration whose receiving fields are not named anywhere in the record. Schreiber Farm in Greenville, 12 km from the divide, holds its registrations (land application, transfer, storage) in its own name at one address, so it is the one receiving farm the map can place — not the only one that exists. No PFAS test of any of these fields or their runoff is in the record.

*Not measured from industry here.* This is a gap in the rules, not a clean bill. Factories report PFAS to EPA's Toxics Release Inventory only if they make, process or use more than 100 lb of a listed PFAS in a year and belong to a covered industry, and only since 2020; within 30 miles, one Albany facility has ever filed, reporting zero release of a perfluoro sulfonic acid salt in 2024. Sewage plants and industrial outfalls test for what their permits name, and no permit within the study area names a PFAS, so none has ever measured one. Landfill leachate, fire-training grounds and septage fields are not required to test either. Nobody is forbidden to look; nobody is required to.

### What it does
*People.* PFAS do not poison quickly; they accumulate in blood for years and shift the body's chemistry. They are endocrine and metabolic disruptors in the plain sense: they interfere with thyroid hormone, with the liver's handling of fat and cholesterol, and with the immune system's ability to answer a vaccine. In ATSDR's hedged words, the epidemiological evidence "suggests associations" between PFAS exposure and higher cholesterol, lower antibody response to some vaccines, changes in liver enzymes, pregnancy-induced hypertension and preeclampsia, small decreases in birth weight, and (for PFOA) kidney and testicular cancer ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/pfas/health-effects/index.html)); thyroid and fertility associations are reported but inconsistent across studies ([ATSDR profile](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxprofiles/tp200.pdf)). Drinking water is the main route where a well is contaminated; the federal limit of 4 ng/L is set on the immune and developmental endpoints, not cancer. At Cairo's 6.75 ng/L the question is chronic, not acute: nobody gets sick from a glass, and everyone who drinks it for twenty years carries more of it.

*In the laboratory.* The animal studies are where the mechanism shows. Rats and mice dosed with PFOS or PFOA grow enlarged livers with swollen cells and proliferating peroxisomes; their pups are born small and fewer survive; their thymus and spleen shrink and their antibody response to a challenge collapses; long-term PFOA produces liver, testicular Leydig-cell and pancreatic tumors, and long-term PFOS liver tumors ([ATSDR profile](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxprofiles/tp200.pdf)). The doses are far above anything in a Cairo tap, and rodents clear PFAS in days where people take decades, which is the reason the human limits are set in parts per trillion: the same body burden takes a rat a large dose and a person a small one, held for years.

*Plants and animals.* PFAS move from soil and irrigation water into plants — leafy vegetables and grasses take up the short-chain compounds most readily — and from grass into milk and meat; dairy herds on sludge-amended fields elsewhere in the Northeast have been quarantined for it (general literature; no local test). In wildlife the same blood-chemistry and immune effects appear in fish, otters and birds, and PFOS concentrates up the food chain the way mercury does, though less steeply. No local fish, milk, soil or crop has been tested for PFAS; the septage fields at Greenville are the obvious place to start.

### What is not known
Where Cairo's PFOS comes from; whether Greenville's wells have been tested (the 2025 report has no PFAS line, and New York requires the test, so either it was not detected and left off, or not yet sampled — ask the district); whether any plateau private well has ever been tested; what the septage fields carry. Rensselaerville's answer is in the table: tested on a three-year cycle, not detected. The 2024 Cairo report exists but is a scanned image and has not been read.

## MICROCYSTIN

### What it is
Microcystin is a liver toxin made by cyanobacteria ("blue-green algae"), the bacteria that turn a lake pea-green in late summer. It is not a pollutant anyone discharges; the lake makes it, from phosphorus, warmth and still water. It is dangerous to dogs that drink bloom water and to people who swallow it; skin contact causes rashes. The numbers: DEC calls a bloom *confirmed* at 4 µg/L and *confirmed with high toxins* at 20 µg/L on a shoreline sample or 10 in open water ([DEC HABs program guide](https://dec.ny.gov/sites/default/files/2025-05/habsprogramguide_2025.pdf)); EPA's swimming criterion is 8 µg/L ([EPA 2019](https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/epa-issues-recommendations-recreational-water-quality-criteria-and-swimming-advisories)); for drinking water EPA advises no more than 0.3 µg/L over ten days for children under six and 1.6 for everyone else ([EPA 2015](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2017-06/documents/microcystins-report-2015.pdf)).

### How it gets here
*Phosphorus off the land.* The 2013 TMDL puts Basic Creek Reservoir's phosphorus at about 940 kg a year, three-quarters of it from agriculture and a third of the total arriving underground through shale and till rather than in streams. A reservoir with a five-week residence time barely buffers what the land sends it.

*Phosphorus off the bottom.* Basic Creek is 3.5–4 m deep and does not stratify, yet its bottom water went to 0.04 mg/L of oxygen in July 2013 and 0.91 in August 2018 while iron and manganese rose. Oxygen-starved sediment releases phosphorus back into the water on any hot, still week — the internal load the TMDL set at zero.

*Warmth and calm.* Blooms here run June to November and peak in late September and October, after the water has been warm for months. Every bloom year in the record is also a year with a late, warm autumn; that is a theory the climate register can test.

*Who watches which lake.* Lake Myosotis is a regulated bathing beach (Huyck Preserve, Albany County Health), and DOH's beach table shows it closed for 21 days in 2025 for a bloom ([NYSDOH](https://www.health.ny.gov/environmental/water/drinking/bluegreenalgae/beachdata.htm)); Thompsons Lake (Thacher State Park beach) and Lawson Lake (county park beach) are the other two regulated beaches on this list. Basic Creek and Alcove are watched by the Albany Water Board as a supply, not as a beach. Warners, Onderdonk and Sleepy Hollow depend on lake-association volunteers and DEC's CSLAP program.

### What it does
*People.* Microcystin is a liver toxin; at the doses that come from swallowing bloom water it causes "abdominal pain, headache, sore throat, vomiting and nausea, dry cough, diarrhea, blistering around the mouth, and pneumonia" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/cyanohabs/health-effects-cyanotoxins)); skin contact brings rashes and eye irritation, and spray from a bloom can be inhaled. Chronic low-dose exposure through drinking water is the reason for the 0.3 µg/L ten-day advisory for young children. Children swallow more water swimming than adults do, which is why the recreational numbers are set with them in mind. Nobody drinks Basic Creek Reservoir untreated; Albany's 2019 entry-point tests found none.

*Plants and animals.* Dogs are the usual casualty: they drink from the scum and lick it off their fur, and "animals can get very sick or even die within hours" ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/harmful-algal-blooms/about/index.html)); livestock watering at a bloom edge face the same. Fish and invertebrates die less from the toxin than from the bloom itself — when it collapses and rots, oxygen goes to zero and everything that breathes water suffocates ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/cyanohabs/health-effects-cyanotoxins)); Basic Creek's bottom water at 0.04 mg/L in July 2013 is that event in progress. Crops irrigated with bloom water take microcystin up into leaves and roots and grow more slowly (general literature; no local test) — relevant to any garden hose that draws from a pond in September.

### What is not known
Whether the 2025 blooms carried toxin above the confirmed line — DEC classed every 2025 report as *confirmed* (lab-verified cyanobacteria), none as *high toxins*, but no toxin value from Basic Creek exists after 2016; whether Alcove, Albany's primary supply, has bloomed with toxin (one clean sample, 2016; confirmed blooms every year 2020–2025); what the sewage plants add to Alcove.
## PB

### What it is
Lead is a soft, heavy metal; "lead is an element, so it does not break down" and it "can often stick to soil particles" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=93&toxid=22)). It is a trace constituent of bedrock here, but lead at the tap comes from plumbing: service lines, solder and brass fittings, which corrode into water standing in the pipe overnight ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/basic-information-about-lead-drinking-water)). The drinking-water number is an action level, not a limit: 15 µg/L at the 90th percentile of homes sampled, above which a system must act ([EPA Lead and Copper Rule](https://www.epa.gov/dwreginfo/lead-and-copper-rule)). The health goal is zero ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)). The 2024 revision lowers the action level to 10 µg/L, requires lead service lines replaced within ten years, and samples the first and fifth litre, counting the higher ([EPA LCRI](https://www.epa.gov/node/292882)). For streams the chronic aquatic criterion is 2.5 µg/L at 100 mg/L hardness, lower in softer water ([EPA criteria table](https://www.epa.gov/wqc/national-recommended-water-quality-criteria-aquatic-life-criteria-table)).

### How it gets here
*From the pipe, not the reservoir.* The City of Albany's system, fed by Alcove and Basic Creek, is the local case. Its 90th-percentile result was 13.9 µg/L in 2018 (4 of 55 homes over 15), 13.13 in 2021 (7 of 75 over), and 16.6 in 2023, when 7 of 66 homes exceeded the action level and the highest single tap read 196 µg/L. That is not an MCL violation; it triggers the rule's corrosion-control and notice steps. July–December 2025 was 12.8. Soft, low-mineral water is more corrosive to plumbing ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/basic-information-about-lead-drinking-water)), and reservoir water off the plateau is soft.

*The small districts.* Rensselaerville's 90th percentile was 9.3 µg/L in 2023 (five homes), Catskill's 9.6 in 2021, Greenville's 3 in 2025, Cairo's below 1.01 in 2022. Every one is finished water (tap), and every one is below 15.

*In the creeks.* Of 82 raw surface-water rows since 2001, 38 are not detected (< limit) and the median detection is 0.62 µg/L. The outliers are old: Fox Creek at 26.3 µg/L total in April 2001 and Coeymans Creek at 4.9 in 2003. Those are total-fraction results, lead on spring sediment. Industry within 50 miles reports small air releases; the Ravena cement plant peaked at about 20,200 kg in 2006 and reported 6.5 kg in 2024.

### What it does
*People.* "The nervous system is the main target for lead poisoning in children and adults" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=93&toxid=22)). In children, lower exposures can "decrease mental development, especially learning, intelligence, and behavior"; in adults it raises blood pressure and damages kidneys. "No safe blood lead level in children has been identified," and CDC's reference value for follow-up is 3.5 µg/dL of blood ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/lead-prevention/about/index.html)). Lead stored in a mother's bones is released in pregnancy and crosses the placenta ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/basic-information-about-lead-drinking-water)). EPA calls lead "a probable human carcinogen" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=93&toxid=22)). Symptoms are absent until damage is done; the test is a child's blood.

*Plants and animals.* "Uptake of lead by plants is limited by its generally low availability in soils," and lead does not biomagnify up food chains ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/5211200)). The wildlife route is swallowed metal: loons that take lost fishing sinkers and eagles that eat bullet fragments in carcasses show "incoordination, weakness, drooped wings" and die within weeks ([Cornell Wildlife Health Lab](https://cwhl.vet.cornell.edu/system/files/public/cwhl-fact-sheets-lead.pdf)). New York restricts small lead sinkers for that reason.

### What is not known
Whether any plateau private well has been sampled at the tap; the record holds none, and a bedrock well with old brass or solder is the same plumbing question as a city house. Which Albany homes exceeded in 2023, and whether the 2025 first-half figure (7.38) or the second half (12.8) is the trend.

## AS

### What it is
Arsenic is "a naturally occurring element widely distributed in the earth's crust" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=19&toxid=3)); U.S. soils average 7.2 µg/g with a range below 0.1 to 97 ([ATSDR ToxGuide](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxguides/toxguide-2.pdf)). It "cannot be destroyed in the environment. It can only change its form" and has no taste or smell. The drinking-water MCL is 10 µg/L, in force since January 23, 2006, when it replaced a 50 µg/L standard ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/node/120901)); the health goal is zero ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)). The chronic aquatic criterion is 150 µg/L ([EPA criteria table](https://www.epa.gov/wqc/national-recommended-water-quality-criteria-aquatic-life-criteria-table)).

### How it gets here
*Out of the rock, when oxygen runs out.* "Arsenic occurs naturally as a trace component in many rocks and sediments," and whether it dissolves depends on redox and pH; in the northern glacial aquifer system, low-oxygen water is where it moves ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/arsenic-and-drinking-water)). About 7 percent of sampled U.S. wells exceed 10 µg/L. The valley-fill wells at Cairo are reducing, with iron, manganese and methane, and Cairo's reserve Well #3 read 2.7 µg/L in March 2018, the only finished-water (tap) arsenic result in the record. A 2008 USGS survey of the lower Hudson basin found one of 32 wells over the standard ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/groundwater-quality-lower-hudson-river-basin-new-york-2008)).

*The local wells.* Of 120 raw groundwater rows from 1971 to 2021, 38 are not detected (< limit) and the median detection is 2.6 µg/L. The highest water result is 16.4 µg/L total, in a USGS well sampled August 2006 in the Catskill foothills; two 1972–73 results of exactly 10 µg/L look like an old reporting limit. Streambed sediment near Albany held 154 mg/kg in 2018, which is rock, not water.

*Lakes and creeks.* Raw lake water runs 1 to 4 µg/L total: Sleepy Hollow Lake 4 µg/L in October 2009, Basic Creek Reservoir 2.6 in September 2018. Creek water is lower, with a median of 1.2 µg/L and 12 non-detects in 39 rows; Hannacrois Creek's 1.5 in July 2022 is the highest. A reservoir bottom at 0.04 mg/L of oxygen is where sediment arsenic re-dissolves, the same chemistry that releases phosphorus.

*Orchards.* Lead arsenate was the orchard spray of the early twentieth century (general literature; no fetched source), and EPA lists "runoff from orchards" as a source ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)). Arsenic "tends to concentrate and remain in upper soil layers indefinitely" ([ATSDR ToxGuide](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxguides/toxguide-2.pdf)). No old-orchard soil in the Hilltowns has been tested.

### What it does
*People.* "Ingesting or breathing low levels of inorganic arsenic for a long time can cause a darkening of the skin and the appearance of small 'corns' or 'warts' on the palms, soles, and torso," and "can increase the risk of skin cancer and cancer in the liver, bladder, and lungs" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=19&toxid=3)). "Long-term exposure to arsenic in children may result in lower IQ scores." USGS notes elevated bladder cancer in northern New England with private-well arsenic identified as a contributing factor ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/arsenic-and-drinking-water)). Nothing in the local record is above 10 µg/L except that one 2006 well.

*Plants and animals.* Soluble arsenic "may be carried long distances" in water and adsorbs onto sediment ([ATSDR ToxGuide](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxguides/toxguide-2.pdf)). "Fish and shellfish can accumulate arsenic; most of this arsenic is in an organic form called arsenobetaine that is much less harmful" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=19&toxid=3)). Local creek and lake levels sit two orders of magnitude under the 150 µg/L chronic criterion for aquatic life.

### What is not known
Whether any plateau private well has been tested; the USGS wells are scattered, most sampled once. Cairo's Well #3 has one result, from 2018, and the 2024 report is an unread scan. Rensselaerville's and Greenville's reports carry no arsenic line. No Basic Creek Reservoir arsenic result exists after September 2018.

## RADON

### What it is
Radon-222 is "a naturally occurring, radioactive, noble gas that is odorless, colorless, and tasteless," formed in the decay chain that begins with uranium ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxfaqs/tfacts145.pdf)). Its half-life is 3.8 days ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radiation/radionuclide-basics-radon)), so it never accumulates; the rock makes it continuously and it decays as fast as it arrives. The standard that matters is for air: EPA's action level for indoor radon is 4 pCi/L ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radon/health-risk-radon)). For water there is only a 1999 proposal: an MCL of 300 pCi/L, or 4,000 pCi/L for a system that runs an indoor-air mitigation program ([EPA proposed rule](https://archive.epa.gov/water/archive/web/html/fact.html)). It was never finalized. Radon is excluded from the gross-alpha standard and needs its own test ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/dwreginfo/radionuclides-rule)).

### How it gets here
*Up from the shale.* "Radon gas released from rocks and soil can move to air, groundwater, and surface water" ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxfaqs/tfacts145.pdf)). A bedrock well pulls water that has sat in uranium-bearing shale; a surface reservoir has already vented its radon to the sky. USGS sampled 317 New York wells from 2003 to 2008 and found about 8 percent of bedrock wells above eight times the proposed limit, against 2 percent of sand-and-gravel wells ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70040137)). In the lower Hudson basin in 2008, 17 of 32 wells exceeded 300 pCi/L and 2 exceeded 4,000, with a maximum of 13,800 in crystalline bedrock ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/groundwater-quality-lower-hudson-river-basin-new-york-2008)).

*The local wells.* All 56 raw groundwater results, 1993 to 2021, are detections; the median is 90 pCi/L, and the plateau bedrock wells run 360 to 1,830. The highest is a USGS well sampled July 2013 at 1,830 pCi/L; a 1994 well reached 1,400, a 2018 well 900, a 2021 well 430. None reaches 4,000; about half exceed the proposed 300.

*From the tap to the room.* "Radon in water can become airborne especially when the water is used for cooking or showering" ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxfaqs/tfacts145.pdf)). The rule of thumb is that 10,000 pCi/L in water adds about 1 pCi/L to indoor air under normal household use ([NYSDOH Wadsworth Center](https://www.wadsworth.org/node/61227/printable/print)). By that ratio the highest well on record adds roughly 0.2 pCi/L to a house, a fraction of the 4 pCi/L action level; the soil under the slab is the larger source. No district reports radon, because no rule requires it; only community supplies are required to test for radioactivity at all ([Wadsworth](https://www.wadsworth.org/node/61227/printable/print)).

### What it does
*People.* "Long-term exposure to elevated levels of radon progeny in air increases your chance of getting lung cancer," and "cigarette smoking greatly increases your chance of developing lung cancer if you are exposed to radon" ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxfaqs/tfacts145.pdf)). EPA estimates about 21,000 U.S. lung cancer deaths a year from radon, roughly 2,900 among people who never smoked ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radon/health-risk-radon)). For the water route specifically, EPA's proposal estimated about 168 cancer deaths a year nationally, 89 percent from breathing radon released from water and the rest, mainly stomach cancer, from drinking it ([EPA proposed rule](https://archive.epa.gov/water/archive/web/html/fact.html)). Children's "smaller lungs and faster breathing rates may result in higher radiation doses" ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxfaqs/tfacts145.pdf)).

*Plants and animals.* Radon passes through living things without being held; a four-day gas does not accumulate in tissue the way its parent radium does. The agency sheets do not address effects on plants or wildlife, and no local study exists (general literature; no fetched source).

### What is not known
The USGS wells are identified by number, not town, so which hamlets sit on the 1,830 pCi/L rock is not in the record. No private well has a paired indoor-air test, which is the measurement that matters. Rensselaerville, Greenville and Cairo have never reported radon, and the Rensselaerville and Greenville wells are the bedrock type USGS found highest.

## RADIUM

### What it is
Radium is "a naturally occurring radioactive metal" formed when uranium and thorium decay in rock, and as it decays "it creates a radioactive gas, radon" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radiation/radionuclide-basics-radium)). Radium-226, from the uranium chain, has a half-life of 1,600 years; radium-228, from the thorium chain, 5.75 years. Chemically it behaves like calcium and goes to bone (general literature; no fetched source). The drinking-water MCL is 5 pCi/L for the two isotopes combined, with a separate 15 pCi/L limit on gross alpha activity that excludes radon and uranium ([EPA Radionuclides Rule](https://www.epa.gov/dwreginfo/radionuclides-rule)); the health goal is zero and the listed effect is "increased risk of cancer" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)).

### How it gets here
*From the rock, under three conditions.* USGS sampled principal aquifers nationally and found radium above 5 pCi/L most often in "(1) oxygen-poor water, (2) acidic conditions (low pH), and (3) high concentrations of dissolved solids" ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/2010/3113/)). The worst are the Midwest's Cambro-Ordovician sandstones and the Atlantic Coastal Plain sands, where over 20 percent of wells exceed the limit; "about 98 percent of the wells that contained combined radium at concentrations greater than the MCL were east of the High Plains." The Hilltowns plateau does not fit the profile. Its bedrock wells are soft sodium-bicarbonate water, neither acidic nor salty. The reducing valley-fill wells at Cairo meet the first condition only.

*The local numbers.* Eight raw groundwater results, from three USGS wells near Albany sampled in 2006, run from 0.01 to 0.81 pCi/L dissolved, median 0.24. Two finished-water (tap) results exist: Greenville Water District No. 1 reported 0.82 pCi/L on October 13, 2022, judged against 5, which its report calls a bedrock signal; the City of Albany's 2025 measurements list a weekly mean of 0.54 pCi/L reported against the 15 pCi/L gross-alpha line. Every result on record is under a fifth of the standard.

*Radium as the parent.* The radon in a bedrock well is made by radium in the rock, not by radium in the water. A well can carry 1,830 pCi/L of radon and under 1 pCi/L of radium, which is what the plateau record shows. They are tested by different methods; one does not predict the other.

### What it does
*People.* "Exposure to high levels of radium results in an increased incidence of bone, liver, and breast cancer"; EPA and the National Academy of Sciences class it "a known human carcinogen" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=790&toxid=154)). The evidence is historical: radium was painted on watch dials until the 1960s, and patients once injected with it later showed anemia, cataracts, broken teeth and stunted growth ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=790&toxid=154)). Those doses were far above anything in a well. Everyone meets "low levels of radium in the air, water, and food," and contaminated well water is one of the listed routes ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=790&toxid=154)). At Greenville's 0.82 pCi/L the question is not health but whether the number changes.

*Plants and animals.* Radium in soil "may strongly attach to those materials" and "may be absorbed by plants"; it "may concentrate in fish and other aquatic organisms" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=790&toxid=154); [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radiation/radionuclide-basics-radium)). Because it substitutes for calcium, it goes to bone and shell in animals as it does in people (general literature; no fetched source). No local fish, soil or plant has been analysed for it.

### What is not known
Whether Rensselaerville, Cairo or Catskill have ever reported radium; their annual reports carry no line for it, so a blank may mean not detected or not yet sampled in the reporting cycle. No private well on the plateau has a radium result. The 2006 USGS wells are near Albany, not in the Hilltowns, and have not been resampled.

## BA

### What it is
Barium is "a silvery-white metal which exists in nature only in ores containing mixtures of elements" ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxfaqs/tfacts24.pdf)). Its common minerals, barium sulfate (barite) and barium carbonate, "do not dissolve well in water" and "can last a long time in the environment," while the soluble salts "usually do not last in these forms for a long time" before they precipitate again. It does not degrade. The drinking-water MCL is 2 mg/L (2,000 µg/L), the health goal the same, and the listed effect "increase in blood pressure" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)). EPA "determined that barium is not likely to be carcinogenic to humans following ingestion" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=326&toxid=57)).

### How it gets here
*Barite, minus sulfate.* Barium in bedrock water is held in check by sulfate: as long as sulfate is present, barite stays solid. Where sulfate-reducing bacteria strip the sulfate out, the mineral keeps dissolving. That is the mechanism USGS documented in Devonian shale at Cattaraugus, southwestern New York, where groundwater reached 23 mg/L, "the highest found reported from any natural ground-water system in the world" at the time ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/wri844129)). Devonian shale is also the bedrock of the Catskill front here (general literature; no fetched source), and Cairo's reducing, methane-bearing valley-fill wells are the sulfate-poor water the mechanism needs.

*The local wells.* All 128 raw groundwater rows since 1971 are detections; the median is 260 µg/L, roughly an eighth of the MCL. Three USGS wells exceed it: 73,000 µg/L dissolved in a well sampled August 1992 in the Catskills west of the study area, 2,920 recoverable in a July 2021 well, and 2,100 dissolved in a September 1992 well logged to the Devonian System. By their coordinates the two 1992 wells sit under a mile apart. USGS's 2008 lower-Hudson survey found a median of 50.6 µg/L in 32 wells, none over the limit ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/groundwater-quality-lower-hudson-river-basin-new-york-2008)).

*At the tap.* Every district reports it. Greenville Water District No. 1 reported 0.183 mg/L in March 2025, the highest finished-water (tap) result and about a tenth of the limit; Cairo 0.016 in 2021–22; Catskill 0.01 in 2022; Rensselaerville 0.0082 in 2025; the City of Albany 0.0049 in June 2025 and 0.004 in 2019. The ordering is the geology: Greenville's bedrock wells first, the reservoir systems last. Industrial releases within 50 miles are small; the Watervliet Arsenal reported 0.65 kg to water in 2024.

### What it does
*People.* "Ingesting drinking water containing levels of barium above the EPA drinking water guidelines for relatively short periods of time can cause gastrointestinal disturbances and muscle weakness"; very large doses of the soluble salts "can cause changes in heart rhythm or paralysis and possibly death" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=326&toxid=57)). Blood pressure may go up or down. The insoluble sulfate, the form swallowed for a contrast X-ray, is "not generally harmful" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=326&toxid=57)). Nothing at a Hilltowns tap approaches the 2 mg/L standard.

*Plants and animals.* "Animals that drank barium over long periods had damage to the kidneys, decreases in body weight, and some died" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=326&toxid=57)). "Fish and aquatic organisms can accumulate barium" ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxfaqs/tfacts24.pdf)). In a stream, dissolved barium meets sulfate and drops out as barite in the sediment; the 361 mg/kg in an Albany-area streambed in 2018 is that mineral. No aquatic criterion exists for it.

### What is not known
Whether the 73,000 µg/L well of 1992 was ever resampled, or was a domestic supply. No plateau private well has barium paired with sulfate, the pair that would show whether the Cattaraugus mechanism operates here. Greenville's 0.183 mg/L is reported as a range top; the low end and the sampling dates behind it are not in the record.
## FE

### What it is
Iron is the second most abundant metal in the Earth's crust, about 5% of it ([WHO](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/wash-documents/wash-chemicals/iron-background-document.pdf)). It is an essential nutrient. In water it has two faces. With oxygen present it is rust, a solid that settles out. Without oxygen it dissolves as iron(II), and "concentrations will usually be 0.5–10 mg/litre, but concentrations up to 50 mg/litre can sometimes be found" in anaerobic groundwater ([WHO](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/wash-documents/wash-chemicals/iron-background-document.pdf)). Two numbers govern it. The drinking-water standard is a secondary one, 300 µg/L, set for "rusty color; sediment; metallic taste; reddish or orange staining," not for health ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/secondary-drinking-water-standards-guidance-nuisance-chemicals)). For stream life the chronic criterion is 1,000 µg/L, from 1986 ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/wqc/national-recommended-water-quality-criteria-aquatic-life-criteria-table)).

### How it gets here
*Out of rock, when the oxygen goes.* Every aquifer here holds iron; what varies is whether it dissolves. Plateau bedrock wells are oxygen-bearing and read low: the median of 273 groundwater results (1967–2021) is 50.9 µg/L, and 12 are not detected. Valley-fill wells at Cairo are reducing and carry 879 µg/L, with manganese and methane. The extremes are Hudson-valley wells in artificial fill and alluvium sampled in 1974–75: 2,300,000 µg/L dissolved in one, 32,000 and 24,000 in two others, and 14,000 µg/L in a Greene County well in 1992.

*Off the bottom of lakes.* Basic Creek Reservoir loses its bottom oxygen in hot weeks (0.04 mg/L, July 2013), and iron comes off the sediment when it does. The lake record (33 results, 2009–2023, median 201 µg/L) peaks at Sleepy Hollow Lake, 2,750 µg/L in October 2009; Vly Creek Reservoir 1,760 in August 2016; Potic Reservoir 950 in June 2023; Lawson Lake 941 in August 2015.

*In creeks, mostly as silt.* Creek results are total iron, so they rise with mud. Fox Creek reached 7,120 µg/L in April 2001 and Coeymans Creek 2,520 in September 2003; the median of 69 creek results is 288 µg/L.

*From outfalls.* Hannay Reels discharges to upper Basic Creek 8.5 km from the reservoir under a 1 mg/L limit; its FY2026 median was 0.624 mg/L (max 1.08), and the median was over the limit in 2009, 2014, 2016, 2024 and 2025. Haleon Oak Hill's outfall to Catskill Creek fell from a median of 420 µg/L in FY2009 to 50 in FY2026 (limit 440).

### What it does
*People.* WHO proposes "no health-based guideline value for iron"; the concern at well levels is nuisance. Taste "is not usually noticeable at iron concentrations below 0.3 mg/litre," and "laundry and sanitary ware will stain" above it ([WHO](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/wash-documents/wash-chemicals/iron-background-document.pdf)). Iron also feeds "iron bacteria," the orange slime in well casings. Finished water (tap) runs low: Albany's 2025 monthly mean was 59.9 µg/L, with one month at 338, above 300 once; Rensselaerville measured 0.0558 mg/L in December 2021. Cairo's raw well water starts at 879 µg/L.

*Plants and animals.* Iron in a creek is judged at 1,000 µg/L chronic. Fox Creek in 2001 and Coeymans Creek in 2003 and 2013 exceeded that on single days; a flood-day total is mostly particles. In a reservoir bottom the same iron that dissolves in the dark carries phosphorus with it; when the water mixes, the iron re-precipitates and the phosphorus feeds the bloom.

### What is not known
No plateau private well in Westerlo, Berne or Rensselaerville has an iron result in the public record; the 273 groundwater rows are USGS wells, most of them in the valleys. Basic Creek Reservoir's bottom-water iron is described in the DO profiles but has no numeric line since 2018. Cairo's report gives raw iron but not the treated result.

## MN

### What it is
Manganese is iron's companion metal, found in the same rocks and dissolving under the same low-oxygen conditions. It is "an essential nutrient, and eating a small amount of it each day is important to stay healthy" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=101&toxid=23)). Three numbers govern it. The secondary standard is 50 µg/L, for "black to brown color; black staining; bitter metallic taste" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/secondary-drinking-water-standards-guidance-nuisance-chemicals)). EPA's lifetime health advisory is 300 µg/L, with a one-day and ten-day advisory of 1 mg/L, except that "for infants younger than 6 months, the lifetime HA of 0.3 mg/L be used even for an acute exposure of 10 days" ([EPA 2004](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2014-09/documents/support_cc1_magnese_dwreport_0.pdf)). New York's guidance value is the same 300.

### How it gets here
*Reducing groundwater.* A national USGS study of 43,334 wells tied elevated manganese to "shallow, anoxic water tables and soils enriched in organic carbon," found it "higher near rivers than farther from rivers," and estimated that 2.6 million people "potentially consume groundwater with elevated manganese concentrations" ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/elevated-manganese-concentrations-united-states-groundwater-role-land-surface-soil)). Cairo's valley-fill wells at 744 µg/L are that geology. The 273 groundwater results (1967–2021) have a median of 55 µg/L and 14 non-detects; the highs are the same Hudson-valley fill wells as iron, 260,000 µg/L dissolved in 1975, and a Greene County well at 8,700 µg/L in 1992.

*Lake bottoms in summer.* Manganese leaves sediment before iron does, at a milder oxygen shortage (general literature; no fetched source). Basic Creek's bottom water carries about 200 µg/L when oxygen falls. The lake record (32 results, 2009–2023, median 58) is led by Potic Reservoir, 3,050 µg/L in June 2023, Vly Creek Reservoir at 2,670 in September 2016 and 2,010–2,160 the same summer, and Sleepy Hollow Lake at 1,070 in October 2009.

*Creeks, lightly.* Creek water is oxygenated and reads low: 52 results (2001–2021), median 28.5 µg/L, highest Fox Creek 178 in April 2001 and Hannacrois Creek 87 in October 2021.

*Industry, small.* Owens Corning at Feura Bush reported 9.5 kg to air in 2024; Oldcastle Precast at Selkirk 2,805 kg on-site in 2019. The Coeymans leachate plant discharges 0.02 mg/L against a 2 mg/L limit.

### What it does
*People.* At the levels in a stained sink the concern is staining. At hundreds of µg/L over a lifetime, "the nervous system has been determined to be the primary target organ" ([EPA 2004](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2014-09/documents/support_cc1_magnese_dwreport_0.pdf)); workers breathing it develop manganism, with movements "that may become slow and clumsy." For children ATSDR keeps its hedge: studies "have suggested that extremely high levels of manganese exposure may produce undesirable effects on brain development," and "we do not know for certain that these changes were caused by manganese alone" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=101&toxid=23)). Formula-fed infants under six months are the group the 300 µg/L line protects. Finished water (tap) is far under it: Albany's 2025 monthly mean was 4.16 µg/L, with a single 23 in December 2022; Catskill measured 8 µg/L in April 2019.

*Plants and animals.* In lakes, "manganese tends to attach to particles in the water or settle into the sediment" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=101&toxid=23)), so it cycles between mud and water with the oxygen rather than moving up the food chain. The workbook lists no aquatic criterion for it.

### What is not known
Cairo's report gives raw manganese at 744 µg/L; the treated value delivered to taps has not been read. No plateau private well in the record has a manganese result; the USGS study puts the risk in the shallow near-river wells of the valleys. Basic Creek's bottom-water manganese has no sample after 2018. Potic Reservoir's 3,050 µg/L in June 2023 is a single raw sample with no treated result on file.

## NA

### What it is
Sodium is the reason plateau water feels slippery. There is no drinking-water MCL. Two guidance lines exist: 20 mg/L "for people on very low sodium diets" and 270 mg/L "for people on moderately restricted sodium diets" ([NYSDOH](https://healthweb-back.health.ny.gov/environmental/water/drinking/salt_drinkingwater.htm)). EPA's 20 mg/L figure was written for "individuals on a very low sodium diet (500 mg/day)" and is a taste guide otherwise: sodium is generally not tasted until 30–60 mg/L or more ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sites/production/files/2014-09/documents/support_cc1_sodium_dwreport.pdf)). New York water systems must notify customers above 20.

### How it gets here
*From shale, by exchange.* Water that has sat for years in the Devonian shale under the plateau swaps its calcium for sodium held on clay. The result is soft sodium-bicarbonate water; plateau wells run 109–139 mg/L with little chloride. That is rock, not road, and the chloride entry shows how to tell them apart. Across 270 USGS groundwater results (1967–2021) the median is 8.6 mg/L, but one Greene County well read 6,600 mg/L in August 1992 alongside 13,000 mg/L of chloride, a natural brine, and a Devonian well nearby 1,200 mg/L.

*From road salt.* Sodium chloride is what the plow trucks spread, and sodium follows chloride into ditches and shallow wells. NYSDOH lists "road salt, water softeners, natural salt deposits, sewage and fertilizers" as the ways it rises ([NYSDOH](https://healthweb-back.health.ny.gov/environmental/water/drinking/salt_drinkingwater.htm)). In the Adirondacks, about 40% of groundwater systems sampled exceeded the 20 mg/L notification level ([NYSDEC](https://extapps.dec.ny.gov/docs/administration_pdf/adirondackroadsaltreport.pdf)). Coeymans Creek, which drains the salted valley roads, ran 98.7 mg/L in September 2003 and 44–72 mg/L through that year; the creek median (66 results, 2001–2022) is 25.3 mg/L.

*From softeners.* A salt-regenerating softener sends brine to the septic field; EPA notes softened water itself can exceed 300 mg/L ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sites/production/files/2014-09/documents/support_cc1_sodium_dwreport.pdf)). Lake sodium sits at 27–43 mg/L: Helderberg Lake 43.1 in August 2005, Onderdonk 34.8 the same day, Warners 30.3 in July 2001, from roads and shoreline septic together.

*Industry, historic.* The TRI list is mostly single filings from 1987–88; the one live line is Keymark in Fonda, 1,802 kg to water in 2018, on the Mohawk.

### What it does
*People.* Sodium in water matters only against the salt in food: "most of the salt we consume comes from food," about 3,400 mg a day ([NYSDOH](https://healthweb-back.health.ny.gov/environmental/water/drinking/salt_drinkingwater.htm)). The people the 20 mg/L line is written for have "high blood pressure, or certain heart, kidney or liver diseases" and a doctor's limit on sodium; DOH's advice is to talk to that provider. Finished water (tap) from Albany has hovered at the line: 19.5 mg/L in December 2019, 20.3 in November 2020, 16.8 in 2022, 14.8 in 2023 and 17.8 in June 2025; Catskill measured 18.4 in April 2019. Plateau bedrock wells at 109–139 mg/L are over it by nature.

*Plants and animals.* Sodium on its own does little to stream life; the chloride it arrives with does the damage, and that story is under CL. On land, sodium in salt spray and roadside soil displaces the calcium and magnesium plants need and breaks down soil structure, which is part of why salted verges compact and grow the salt-tolerant weeds DEC's task force describes ([NYSDEC](https://extapps.dec.ny.gov/docs/administration_pdf/adirondackroadsaltreport.pdf)).

### What is not known
Whether plateau well sodium is rising: the 109–139 mg/L values are single samples with no repeat. Which Westerlo and Berne wells within 30 m of a state road have been tested; none is in the record. Rensselaerville's and Greenville's reports carry no sodium line. The 1992 brine well has not been resampled.

## CL

### What it is
Chloride is the half of salt that does the damage. It is conservative: it does not degrade, does not bind to soil, and is not taken up by anything, so what goes on the road ends in groundwater or a creek and stays. Natural background on the plateau is 2–6 mg/L. Three numbers govern it. The drinking-water standard is a secondary one, 250 mg/L, for "salty taste" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/secondary-drinking-water-standards-guidance-nuisance-chemicals)). For stream life EPA's criteria, set in 1988, are 230 mg/L chronic and 860 mg/L acute ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/wqc/national-recommended-water-quality-criteria-aquatic-life-criteria-table)).

### How it gets here
*Road salt, then groundwater.* Deicing is "the dominant source in urban areas of the northern U.S." ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/news/national-news-release/urban-stream-contamination-increasing-rapidly-due-road-salt)); it is the plateau's main source too. In the Adirondack Park, state roads take about 34 tons of salt per lane-mile a year and local roads 13 ([NYSDEC](https://extapps.dec.ny.gov/docs/administration_pdf/adirondackroadsaltreport.pdf)). The rest "infiltrates the groundwater system during the winter and is slowly released to the streams throughout the year," which is why USGS found summer chloride rising in 13 of its northern streams ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/news/national-news-release/urban-stream-contamination-increasing-rapidly-due-road-salt)). Groundwater here: 351 results (1967–2021), median 15 mg/L, 56 not detected. The 13,000 mg/L in a Greene County well in August 1992 and 1,900 in a Devonian well are natural brine, not road; the 1,300 mg/L in a Hudson-valley fill well in 1975 is fill.

*Into the creeks.* Coeymans Creek carries the valley's roads: 161 mg/L in July 2022, 153 in September 2003, 124 in July 2017, all in summer, all groundwater-fed. The creek median (77 results, 2001–2025) is 41.7 mg/L; Basic Creek runs 31–42. Sleepy Hollow Lake read 561 and 397 mg/L in November 2003 and 73–76 in 2017. Onderdonk Lake was 62.7 mg/L in July 2001 and 47.6 at 1.5 m in September 2025; Warners 55.4 in August 2001. The lake median is 24.8.

*Septic and softeners.* Softener brine and household salt both leave through the leach field, which is why shoreline lakes read above background away from roads.

*Industry.* Port Albany Ventures discharges a median 31,200 mg/L brine to the Hudson with no chloride limit; Norlite sends about 270,000 kg a year to the Saw Kill.

### What it does
*People.* Chloride is not a health standard. Above 250 mg/L water tastes salty and corrodes plumbing; the Adirondack survey estimated 31% of homeowners "may be experiencing corrosive damage" from their well water ([NYSDEC](https://extapps.dec.ny.gov/docs/administration_pdf/adirondackroadsaltreport.pdf)). Finished water (tap) from Albany has run 27.7–31.3 mg/L as an annual mean, 2019–2025, against 250; a filtered reservoir supply carrying ten times the plateau background is the road-salt signal.

*Plants and animals.* USGS found 29% of northern urban streams over the 230 mg/L chronic criterion for more than 100 days a year, and "some freshwater organisms are sensitive to chloride" well below it ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/news/national-news-release/urban-stream-contamination-increasing-rapidly-due-road-salt)). DEC's task force reports that salt "reduces the diversity and abundance of zooplankton," that impacts appear "at chloride concentrations between 5 - 90 mg/L," and in soft-water lakes "at concentrations less than 5 mg/L" ([NYSDEC](https://extapps.dec.ny.gov/docs/administration_pdf/adirondackroadsaltreport.pdf)). Sleepy Hollow Lake in November 2003 was over the chronic line. On land the sign is the brown pine and the scorched maple crown: "defoliation, discoloration (yellowing), suppression of flowering, premature mortality, and poor regeneration" along salted roads, and the spread of "non-native vegetation" in the salt strip ([NYSDEC](https://extapps.dec.ny.gov/docs/administration_pdf/adirondackroadsaltreport.pdf)).

### What is not known
No winter sample exists for Basic Creek or its tributaries; every creek result is April–November, and the first melt is when chloride peaks. No plateau private well has a chloride result in the record, so the 2–6 mg/L background is inferred, not measured, for Westerlo. Sleepy Hollow Lake has no chloride sample since 2017.

## NO3

### What it is
Nitrate is nitrogen in its most mobile form, the one plants take up and the one that leaks. It is made in soil from manure, fertilizer, septic effluent and rotting leaves, and it "dissolve[s] easily in water and will therefore move quickly through the soil into surface water and groundwater" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=1186&toxid=258)). Natural background is under 1 mg/L as N. One number governs it: 10 mg/L as N, EPA's enforceable MCL and New York's groundwater standard, with sources listed as "runoff from fertilizer use; leaking from septic tanks, sewage; erosion of natural deposits" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)).

### How it gets here
*Fields.* Fertilizer and manure supply nitrogen the crop cannot all use; the rest "can be washed from farm fields and into waterways during rain events and when snow melts" or "leach through the soil and into groundwater over time" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/nutrientpollution/sources-and-solutions-agriculture)). Coeymans Creek, draining the valley farms, ran 1.37–1.58 mg/L through 2003; the creek median (146 results, 2001–2025) is 207 µg/L, or 0.2 mg/L.

*Septic.* Every plateau house on a leach field sends nitrate down; where the soil stays wet, bacteria strip it back to gas (denitrification), and where it stays dry and oxygenated, nitrate rides to the well. Onderdonk Lake, ringed with camps, read 1,142 µg/L in October 2006, ten times any other lake sample. Schreiber Farm at Greenville takes septage under its own DEC registrations; Becker & Son's Septic Service at Berne is registered to spread it on fields the public list does not name. Farms spreading their own manure need no filing and so appear in no table.

*Sewage plants.* The Schoharie village plant discharged a median 34.6 mg/L in FY2023, about 3,384 kg a year, with no nitrate limit; Windham 6.8.

*Groundwater.* Of 347 groundwater results (1967–2021), 97 are not detected and the median is 0.44 mg/L as N. The highs are one Albany County well cluster in summer 1984: 8,730 µg/L (8.7 mg/L) on 7 August, 4,800 four days later, 3,360 and 3,130 in neighbouring wells. Nothing else in the record approaches the standard. Lakes are lower still: 272 results (1989–2025), median 10 µg/L, 82 not detected, consistent with uptake by algae in the growing season (general literature; no fetched source).

### What it does
*People.* The standard is set for babies. "Infants below the age of six months who drink water containing nitrate in excess of the MCL could become seriously ill and, if untreated, may die. Symptoms include shortness of breath and blue-baby syndrome" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)); the mechanism is methemoglobinemia, blood that cannot carry oxygen, in infants "who drank formula made with water having higher than recommended nitrate" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=1186&toxid=258)). For adults ATSDR reports thyroid changes "in animal studies" and IARC's finding that ingested nitrate and nitrite is "probably carcinogenic to humans" under conditions that form nitrosamines in the stomach; neither EPA nor DHHS has classified it. Finished water (tap) is far below 10: Albany 0.049 mg/L in June 2025, Rensselaerville 0.407 in March 2025, Cairo's NPTP well 0.28 and Well #3 0.03 in June 2022, Catskill 0.08 in February 2022.

*Plants and animals.* Nitrate is fertilizer, and in a lake it fertilizes. With phosphorus it "fuels the growth of harmful algal blooms," and when the bloom dies and decomposes, oxygen falls "to a level that can no longer support aquatic life" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/nutrientpollution/effects-environment)). Basic Creek Reservoir's 2013 TMDL is written for phosphorus, so nitrate is the second lever there, not the first.

### What is not known
No plateau private well has a nitrate result in the public record. The 1984 Albany County wells at 8.7 mg/L have not been resampled. Onderdonk's 2006 spike has no follow-up nitrate sample to say whether it was one autumn or every autumn. The Greenville district report has no nitrate line; Schreiber Farm's fields have no groundwater monitoring on file.
## PFOA

### What it is
PFOA (perfluorooctanoic acid) is the carboxylic twin of PFOS: the same eight-carbon fluorinated chain, ending in a carboxylate group instead of a sulfonate. It does not occur in nature. It is the Hoosick Falls chemical — 662 ng/L in the village's finished water and 620 in its raw supply well in 2015, with 18,000 ng/L in groundwater under the Saint-Gobain plant on McCaffrey Street ([EPA, 2016](https://19january2021snapshot.epa.gov/sites/static/files/2016-01/documents/hoosickfallspresentation.pptx_.pdf)). That plant, 48 miles from Westerlo, appears on this page for its toluene. PFOA leaves the body faster than PFOS but still slowly: elimination half-life 2.1 to 10.1 years ([ATSDR profile](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxprofiles/tp200.pdf)). The numbers: New York's 10 ng/L in tap water since 2020; EPA's 4 ng/L, set in 2024 and kept in May 2025, compliance due 2029 or, under a May 2026 proposal, 2031 ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas)); and DEC's 2021 guidance value of 6.7 ng/L for raw groundwater and surface water, set below the tap standard for "an extra margin of safety" ([NYSDEC](https://dec.ny.gov/news/press-releases/2021/10/dec-releases-new-guidance-to-regulate-pfoa-pfos-and-14-dioxane-in-state-waters)).

See PFOS for the manufacturing history. What differs for PFOA: DuPont, not 3M, was the main maker, using it to polymerise Teflon; it was "phased out between 2006 and 2015 in the United States" under EPA's PFOA Stewardship Program, and GenX is its designated replacement in nonstick coatings and some foams ([EPA, GenX advisory](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2022-06/drinking-water-genx-2022.pdf)). The Hoosick Falls source was a manufacturing plant, not foam.

### Who made it, and who still does
*Discovery.* PFOA comes out of electrochemical fluorination, a process Joseph Simons worked out in the 1930s at Penn State with 3M's money; the work stayed classified through the war because it bore on uranium hexafluoride, and Simons published it in 1949 ([Wikipedia, Simons process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_fluorination)). 3M began making PFOA by that route in 1947 at Cottage Grove, Minnesota, and from 1951 sold it to DuPont, which called it "C8" and used it to make Teflon ([Wikipedia, PFOA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfluorooctanoic_acid)). 3M began mass production of its fluorochemicals at the Cottage Grove "Chemolite" plant in 1949 ([Wikipedia, 3M Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3M_contamination_of_Minnesota_groundwater); [MPCA](https://www.pca.state.mn.us/local-sites-and-projects/east-metro-3m-pfas-contamination)).

*Uses, past and present.* Its main job was as the processing aid that lets PTFE and other fluoropolymers form in water. It also went into non-stick coatings, stain- and water-repellent carpet, textiles and clothing, firefighting foam, floor wax, sealants and grease-proof food paper ([Wikipedia, PFOA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfluorooctanoic_acid)). The Hoosick Falls contamination, 48 miles from Westerlo, came from PTFE-coated fabric work at the Saint-Gobain McCaffrey Street plant and older Honeywell sites; the village water was found over 70 ng/L in 2016 and the site went on the federal Superfund list in July 2017 ([NY DEC](https://www.dec.ny.gov/chemical/108791.html)).

*Makers, then.* 3M announced its phase-out in 2000 and stopped electrochemical PFOA in 2002 ([Wikipedia, PFOA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfluorooctanoic_acid)). In 2006 EPA enrolled the eight remaining producers — Arkema, Asahi, BASF (successor to Ciba), Clariant, Daikin, 3M/Dyneon, DuPont and Solvay Solexis — in the PFOA Stewardship Program: 95 percent cuts by 2010, elimination by 2015. All eight reported meeting it, most by quitting long-chain PFAS or leaving the business ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/assessing-and-managing-chemicals-under-tsca/fact-sheet-20102015-pfoa-stewardship-program)). DuPont's replacement was GenX (HFPO-DA), made at Fayetteville, North Carolina ([EPA GenX advisory](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2022-06/drinking-water-genx-2022.pdf)); that business passed to Chemours when it was spun off on July 1, 2015 ([Chemours 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001627223/000119312515242128/d949816dex991.htm)). Minnesota settled with 3M for $850 million in 2018 ([Minnesota AG](https://www.ag.state.mn.us/Office/Cases/3M/)); in June 2023 3M agreed to pay U.S. water systems at least $10.3 billion ([NGWA](https://www.ngwa.org/detail/news/2023/06/23/3m-reaches-$10.3-billion-settlement-over-pfas-contamination)). Stockholm listed PFOA in Annex A in 2019 ([Stockholm Convention](https://www.pops.int/TheConvention/ThePOPs/TheNewPOPs/tabid/2511/Default.aspx)).

*Makers, now.* Production moved to China. Dongyue Group in Shandong, the world's largest Teflon maker, was estimated in 2016 to release about 63 tonnes of PFOA a year; a second cluster at Changshu, Jiangsu, hosts Solvay, Arkema, Daikin and Chemours plants; Chinese PFOA output roughly tripled between 2004 and 2012 ([The Intercept](https://theintercept.com/2016/09/15/the-teflon-toxin-goes-to-china/)). In December 2022 3M announced it would leave all PFAS manufacturing by the end of 2025, a business with about $1.3 billion in annual sales ([3M](https://news.3m.com/2022-12-20-3M-to-Exit-PFAS-Manufacturing-by-the-End-of-2025)), and its Q3 2025 filing still stated that date ([3M 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/66740/000006674025000089/mmm-20250930.htm)). Five European countries filed a restriction on roughly 10,000 PFAS with ECHA in January 2023 ([ECHA](https://echa.europa.eu/-/echa-publishes-pfas-restriction-proposal)). Locally, Albany's finished water was non-detect in 2020; Catskill read 2.02 ng/L in 2021.

### How it gets here
*Coating plants, then the aquifer.* PFOA was a processing aid, so it left factories in stack air and rinse water and settled around them. Hoosick Falls found it by testing the village wells in summer 2015 ([EPA, 2016](https://19january2021snapshot.epa.gov/sites/static/files/2016-01/documents/hoosickfallspresentation.pptx_.pdf)). Carboxylates sorb less to soil carbon than sulfonates of the same chain length ([ITRC](https://pfas-1.itrcweb.org/5-environmental-fate-and-transport-processes/)), so a PFOA plume runs somewhat ahead of a PFOS plume from the same source.

*Septage and sludge on fields.* The same route as PFOS: Schreiber Farm in Greenville is the one receiving farm the record can place, because it holds its own registrations; Becker & Son's Septic Service in Berne is registered to spread too, on fields the public list does not name. Any septage spreading in New York needs a DEC registration or permit; a farm's own manure does not. No PFAS test of any of these fields exists.

*What the local record shows.* Two results. Village of Catskill's finished water (tap) read 2.02 ng/L on 19 August 2021, against New York's 10 ng/L and below EPA's 4. City of Albany's finished water (tap) at the Feura Bush entry point was not detected (< 0.8–0.82 ng/L) in three quarters of 2020. No raw-water result — well, creek or reservoir — exists for PFOA anywhere in the study area, and no permit or TRI filing within it reports PFOA.

### What it does
*People.* The same profile as PFOS, with one addition. ATSDR's epidemiological review "suggests associations" between PFAS exposure and higher cholesterol, lower antibody response to some vaccines, changes in liver enzymes, pregnancy-induced hypertension and preeclampsia, and small decreases in birth weight; for PFOA specifically it adds kidney and testicular cancer ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/pfas/health-effects/index.html)). EPA's summary adds "decreased fertility" and "increased risk of some cancers" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/pfas/our-current-understanding-human-health-and-environmental-risks-pfas)). At Catskill's 2.02 ng/L the exposure is chronic and low; a 4 ng/L limit is about body burden built over decades.

*Plants and animals.* As for PFOS: PFAS "can build up in people, animals, and the environment over time," and EPA names fish from contaminated water, dairy from exposed livestock, and animals grazing biosolids-amended land as the food routes ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/pfas/our-current-understanding-human-health-and-environmental-risks-pfas)). PFOA accumulates less steeply than PFOS in fish (general literature; no fetched source). No local fish, milk, soil or crop has been tested.

### What is not known
Where Catskill's 2.02 ng/L comes from, and whether it is rising — one sample, one year. Whether Cairo, Greenville or Rensselaerville have a PFOA line in any report; the record holds none. No private well on the plateau has been tested for PFOA.

## PFHXS

### What it is
PFHxS (perfluorohexane sulfonic acid) is PFOS with two carbons removed: a six-carbon fluorinated chain and the same sulfonate head. It does not occur in nature. It was a component and impurity of the firefighting foams (AFFF) made alongside PFOS, and it is the compound that runs ahead of PFOS in a groundwater plume. Its stay in the body is the longest of the common PFAS: elimination half-life 4.7 to 35 years ([ATSDR profile](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxprofiles/tp200.pdf)). The numbers are unsettled. EPA set a 10 ng/L limit in April 2024 and in May 2026 proposed to "rescind drinking water regulations for PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA (commonly known as GenX), and the Hazard Index mixture" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas)). New York has no MCL for it; the state regulates PFOA and PFOS at 10 ng/L and is "working to pass even more drinking water standards and notification levels for up to 23 PFAS" ([NYSDOH](https://www.health.ny.gov/environmental/water/drinking/emerging_pfas_publicwater.htm)). DEC's 2021 ambient guidance names PFOA, PFOS and 1,4-dioxane, not PFHxS ([NYSDEC](https://dec.ny.gov/news/press-releases/2021/10/dec-releases-new-guidance-to-regulate-pfoa-pfos-and-14-dioxane-in-state-waters)). Cairo's 2022 report set its result against 50 ng/L.

See PFOS for the manufacturing history; PFHxS shares it, a 3M product listed by the Stockholm Convention in 2022. It is not one of the short-chain replacements — those are PFBS and PFBA, four carbons.

### Who made it, and who still does
*Discovery.* PFHxS is the six-carbon cousin of PFOS, and it came off the same 3M electrochemical fluorination line that Joseph Simons developed under 3M sponsorship in the 1930s and published in 1949 ([Wikipedia, Simons process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_fluorination)). The process does not make one chain length cleanly; PFHxS was both a product in its own right and an impurity riding along with PFOS. 3M began mass-producing its sulfonyl fluorochemicals at Cottage Grove, Minnesota in 1949 ([Wikipedia, 3M Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3M_contamination_of_Minnesota_groundwater); [MPCA](https://www.pca.state.mn.us/local-sites-and-projects/east-metro-3m-pfas-contamination)).

*Uses, past and present.* Its uses track PFOS's: surfactant in aqueous film-forming firefighting foam, mist suppressant in chrome and other metal plating, water- and oil-repellent finish for textiles, leather and carpet, polishing and cleaning agents, coatings, and semiconductor manufacture ([Stockholm Convention, All POPs](https://chm.pops.int/TheConvention/ThePOPs/AllPOPs/tabid/2509/Default.aspx)). Around 2016 firefighting foam took about two-thirds of what was made and textile finishing about a fifth ([Norwegian Environment Agency](https://www.miljodirektoratet.no/globalassets/publikasjoner/m1388/m1388.pdf)). After PFOS was restricted, some plants switched to PFHxS as the drop-in ([POPRC risk profile, via Japan MOE](https://www.env.go.jp/content/000087991.pdf)). Cairo's water district measured 2.53 ng/L in 2022, the only local detection on record; because it moves faster than PFOS it typically arrives at a well first.

*Makers, then.* 3M was most likely the main manufacturer, with about 227 tonnes made in the United States in 1997, and it stopped making PFHxS and its precursors between 2000 and 2002 ([POPRC risk profile](https://www.env.go.jp/content/000087991.pdf)). Miteni in Italy also produced it, stopped in 2013 and went bankrupt in 2018 ([Norwegian Environment Agency](https://www.miljodirektoratet.no/globalassets/publikasjoner/m1388/m1388.pdf)). A global total of 120 to 1,022 tonnes over 1958–2015 is the best estimate ([Wikipedia, PFHxS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfluorohexanesulfonic_acid)). The Stockholm Convention listed PFHxS under Annex A at COP-10 in 2022 with no exemptions, the strictest listing of the three ([Stockholm Convention](https://www.pops.int/TheConvention/ThePOPs/TheNewPOPs/tabid/2511/Default.aspx)).

*Makers, now.* Production is small and Chinese. Two manufacturers were reported around 2016 with a combined output near 700–750 kg a year, falling below 700 kg by 2017 ([Norwegian Environment Agency](https://www.miljodirektoratet.no/globalassets/publikasjoner/m1388/m1388.pdf)); the POPRC named Hubei Hengxin, Wuhan Defu, Wuhan Yangtze River, Shanghai Vatten and Time Chemical, and recorded Hengxin rising from zero in 2008 to 30 tonnes in 2011 before stopping in 2012 ([POPRC risk profile](https://www.env.go.jp/content/000087991.pdf)). 3M announced in December 2022 that it would exit all PFAS manufacturing by end-2025 ([3M](https://news.3m.com/2022-12-20-3M-to-Exit-PFAS-Manufacturing-by-the-End-of-2025)). The 2023 EU proposal to restrict about 10,000 PFAS covers it ([ECHA](https://echa.europa.eu/-/echa-publishes-pfas-restriction-proposal)). EPA set a 10 ng/L limit in April 2024 and in May 2026 proposed rescinding it ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas)). The stock that matters here is not new production but old foam in fire-station lockers and training grounds.

### How it gets here
*Foam first.* AFFF is "used to extinguish flammable liquid-based fires" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/pfas/our-current-understanding-human-health-and-environmental-risks-pfas)): airports, fuel terminals, military bases, fire-company training days. The Cannon Air Force Base pattern in the PFOS entry is the model.

*Then the plume, sorted by chain length.* Sorption to soil carbon "generally increases with increasing perfluoroalkyl tail length," so shorter chains "are less retarded than their long-chain counterparts" and "are more rapidly transported" ([ITRC](https://pfas-1.itrcweb.org/5-environmental-fate-and-transport-processes/)). A well near a foam source reads PFOS-heavy; a well far downgradient reads PFHxS-heavy, because the PFHxS arrived first. The ratio is a rough odometer.

*The one local number.* Cairo Water District's finished water (tap) read 2.53 ng/L of PFHxS on 13 September 2022, reported as 0.00253 µg/L. Cairo's PFOS is 6.75 ng/L. That ratio, about 0.4, is PFOS-dominant, which on the plume logic above does not read as a distant foam source. One sample from one year does not settle it. Cairo's valley-fill wells are reducing water — iron 879 µg/L, manganese 744 µg/L, methane — an old, slow aquifer, the kind that holds a plume a long time.

### What it does
*People.* The PFHxS-specific human evidence is thinner than for PFOA and PFOS. ATSDR names it in two of its "suggests associations" lines — "lower antibody response to some vaccines" and "changes in liver enzymes" ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/pfas/health-effects/index.html)). Because it stays in blood for decades, a small daily dose becomes a large body burden; Cairo's 2.53 ng/L is a chronic question, not an acute one.

*In the laboratory.* The critical effect ATSDR uses to set PFHxS screening levels is "thyroid follicular epithelial hypertrophy/hyperplasia in rats"; liver weight changes also appear, "less extensively characterized than for longer-chain compounds" ([ATSDR profile](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxprofiles/tp200.pdf)).

*Plants and animals.* The same routes as PFOS: fish, dairy from exposed livestock, animals grazing biosolids-amended land ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/pfas/our-current-understanding-human-health-and-environmental-risks-pfas)). Its shorter chain moves more readily into plant tissue than PFOS (general literature; no fetched source). Nothing local has been tested.

### What is not known
Whether Cairo's PFHxS has been resampled since 2022, and whether Albany, Catskill, Greenville or Rensselaerville have ever reported it — none has a PFHxS line in the record. Whether any fire-training ground or fuel terminal in the Catskill Creek valley has been sampled. No plateau private well has been tested, and if the 2026 rescission goes through, no district will be required to test for it either.

## DIOXANE

### What it is
1,4-Dioxane is a synthetic solvent, "a clear, flammable liquid with a faint, pleasant odor," "completely miscible in water" ([EPA fact sheet](https://www.epa.gov/sites/production/files/2014-03/documents/ffrro_factsheet_contaminant_14-dioxane_january2014_final.pdf)). It does not occur in nature. Ninety percent of its historical use was as a stabiliser for the chlorinated degreaser 1,1,1-trichloroethane (TCA), so it travels with old solvent plumes; it is also a manufacturing by-product in "cosmetics, detergents, and shampoos" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=954&toxid=199)). New York set the country's first drinking-water limit, 1 µg/L, in July 2020 ([NYSDEC](https://dec.ny.gov/news/press-releases/2021/10/dec-releases-new-guidance-to-regulate-pfoa-pfos-and-14-dioxane-in-state-waters)); there is no federal MCL. EPA's figures: the concentration for a one-in-a-million lifetime cancer risk is 0.35 µg/L, and its lifetime non-cancer health advisory is 0.2 mg/L ([EPA fact sheet](https://www.epa.gov/sites/production/files/2014-03/documents/ffrro_factsheet_contaminant_14-dioxane_january2014_final.pdf)) — a 570-fold spread that shows the standard is set on cancer, not on toxicity.

### Who made it, and who still does
*Discovery.* Agostinho Vicente Lourenço, a Portuguese chemist working in Wurtz's Paris laboratory on what he called polyethylene alcohols, first made 1,4-dioxane from diethylene glycol and 1,2-dibromoethane; Wikipedia dates it to 1860, a forensic review to 1863, and Wurtz made it by another route the same year ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1,4-Dioxane); [Martelain 2015](https://ic.unicamp.br/~stolfi/EXPORT/projects/wikipedia/00-DOCS/martelain-1_4-dioxane.pdf); [Academy of Sciences of Lisbon](https://comum.rcaap.pt/bitstreams/9101f17c-3678-48e1-b819-7f48bbd1e0bd/download)). Favorsky worked out the acid dehydration of diethylene glycol in 1906, and that is still the industrial route; commercial production began in 1929 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1,4-Dioxane); [Martelain 2015](https://ic.unicamp.br/~stolfi/EXPORT/projects/wikipedia/00-DOCS/martelain-1_4-dioxane.pdf)).

*Uses, past and present.* From the late 1950s its main job was to stabilise 1,1,1-trichloroethane (methyl chloroform), the degreasing solvent, against corroding aluminium; about 3.5 percent by weight went into the solvent, and by the mid-1980s roughly 90 percent of all dioxane made was used this way ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp187-c5.pdf); [Martelain 2015](https://ic.unicamp.br/~stolfi/EXPORT/projects/wikipedia/00-DOCS/martelain-1_4-dioxane.pdf)). Methyl chloroform depletes ozone, and parties to the Montreal Protocol phased out its consumption by January 1, 1996 ([Environment Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/evaluating-existing-substances/111-trichloroethane1.html)); the stabiliser market went with it. What remains is solvent use in chemical processing, laboratory reagent, plastics and rubber, pesticide formulation and pharmaceuticals ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp187-c5.pdf)). The larger route into homes is unintentional: dioxane forms as a by-product when detergents, foaming agents and emulsifiers are ethoxylated, so it turns up in shampoos, soaps and laundry liquids nobody added it to ([NY DEC](https://dec.ny.gov/environmental-protection/pollution-prevention/household-personal-cosmetic-dioxane-limits); [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/assessing-and-managing-chemicals-under-tsca/risk-evaluation-14-dioxane)). New York's ECL Article 35 amendments capped it at 2 ppm in household cleansing and personal-care products from December 31, 2022, 1 ppm from December 31, 2023, and 10 ppm in cosmetics, with all waivers ending December 30, 2025 ([NY DEC](https://dec.ny.gov/environmental-protection/pollution-prevention/household-personal-cosmetic-dioxane-limits)). BASF responded in 2020 with a "low 1,4-dioxane" surfactant line ([BASF](https://www.basf.com/us/en/media/news-releases/2020/01/basf-expands-low-1-4-dioxane-line-to-help-customers-meet-new-reg)).

*Makers, then.* U.S. production began at scale in 1951 and Japanese production in 1958 ([Martelain 2015](https://ic.unicamp.br/~stolfi/EXPORT/projects/wikipedia/00-DOCS/martelain-1_4-dioxane.pdf)). U.S. output was about 5,400 tonnes in 1977 and 6,800 tonnes in 1982; world capacity was 11,000–14,000 tonnes in 1985 ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp187-c5.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1,4-Dioxane)). By 2002 U.S. production had fallen to the 450–4,500 tonne band, and ATSDR named Dow at Freeport, Texas and Ferro at Baton Rouge, Louisiana as the two domestic makers, with BASF in Germany and Osaka Yuki and Toho Chem in Japan abroad ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp187-c5.pdf)).

*Makers, now.* U.S. manufacture plus import was reported at just over 500 tonnes a year in 2016 ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/assessing-and-managing-chemicals-under-tsca/risk-evaluation-14-dioxane)). Chinese suppliers now list it by the thousand tonnes ([ChemicalBook listing](https://api.chemicalbook.com/ProductDetail_EN_14-dioxane_2352626.htm)), and BASF's German production named by ATSDR continues (general literature on current Ludwigshafen output; no fetched source). EPA found unreasonable risk under TSCA in November 2024 ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/assessing-and-managing-chemicals-under-tsca/risk-evaluation-14-dioxane)). Locally, Curia in Rensselaer reported 18.6 kg to air in 2024, and Albany's finished water was non-detect in 2020.

### How it gets here
*Solvent plumes.* Wherever TCA was used for degreasing metal — machine shops, plating lines, electronics — 1,4-dioxane went into the ground with it. Unlike its host solvent it "does not readily biodegrade," its soil sorption is "weak," and it "is expected to move rapidly from soil to groundwater" ([EPA fact sheet](https://www.epa.gov/sites/production/files/2014-03/documents/ffrro_factsheet_contaminant_14-dioxane_january2014_final.pdf)). "In water, 1,4-dioxane is stable and does not break down" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=954&toxid=199)). So it leads the plume, and it is the part that activated carbon and air stripping do not remove; treatment means advanced oxidation ([EPA fact sheet](https://www.epa.gov/sites/production/files/2014-03/documents/ffrro_factsheet_contaminant_14-dioxane_january2014_final.pdf)).

*Drains.* Shampoo, detergent and dish soap carry it in trace amounts, so it is in every septic leach field and sewage outfall, at levels no one here has measured.

*Industry, reported.* Two facilities within 50 miles file it to TRI. Curia in Rensselaer, 22 miles, reported 18.6 kg to the air in 2024, its highest year in nine of filing. Norlite in Cohoes, 28 miles, reported 0.2 kg to its own site in 2020 and none since. Neither drains toward the Hilltowns.

*The one local test.* City of Albany's finished water (tap) at the Feura Bush entry point was not detected (< 0.0081–0.018 µg/L) in three quarters of 2020, against New York's 1 µg/L. The detection limit was fifty times below the standard. No raw-water result from a well, creek or reservoir exists.

### What it does
*People.* Short-term exposure to vapour causes "irritation of the eyes, nose and throat"; long-term exposure "may cause kidney and liver damage"; and "EPA has classified 1,4-dioxane as 'likely to be carcinogenic to humans' by all routes of exposure" ([EPA fact sheet](https://www.epa.gov/sites/production/files/2014-03/documents/ffrro_factsheet_contaminant_14-dioxane_january2014_final.pdf)). HHS lists it as "reasonably anticipated to be a human carcinogen" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=954&toxid=199)). The 1 µg/L standard is about three times EPA's one-in-a-million level, a lifetime-of-drinking calculation.

*In the laboratory.* In rats and mice, inhaled vapour "affects mainly the nasal cavity, liver, and kidneys," and long-term dosing produced nasal and liver cancers ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=954&toxid=199)).

*Plants and animals.* Because it does not bioaccumulate and does not sorb, 1,4-dioxane passes through fish and plants rather than building up in them; DEC's aquatic guidance values sit in the tens of thousands of µg/L, four orders of magnitude above the human-health figure ([NYSDEC](https://dec.ny.gov/news/press-releases/2021/10/dec-releases-new-guidance-to-regulate-pfoa-pfos-and-14-dioxane-in-state-waters)). Wildlife is not the concern here; groundwater is.

### What is not known
Only Albany's 2020 test is on record. New York requires every public system to test, so Rensselaerville, Greenville, Cairo and Catskill either did not detect it and left it off, or their reports were not read for it — ask the district. No private well on the plateau has been tested, and no old machine shop or dry-cleaner site in the Catskill Creek valley has been sampled for it.

## TOLUENE

### What it is
Toluene is the simplest methylated benzene, a clear liquid that "occurs naturally in crude oil and in the tolú tree" and is refined into gasoline, paint, lacquer, adhesives and nail polish ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=160&toxid=29)). The workbook classes it as synthetic because every gram in our air and water was refined or manufactured. Three numbers apply: EPA's drinking-water MCL of 1 mg/L (1,000 µg/L), set against "nervous system, kidney, or liver problems" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)); New York's groundwater and tap standard of 5 µg/L, the "principal organic contaminant" rule applied to any solvent-class compound ([NYSDEC TOGS 1.1.1](https://extapps.dec.ny.gov/docs/water_pdf/togs111.pdf)); and New York's aquatic ambient value of 6,000 µg/L. The 200-fold gap is the difference between a health calculation and a policy that no solvent belongs in groundwater.

### Who made it, and who still does
*Discovery.* Pelletier and Walter isolated it from pine-oil distillate in 1837 and called it rétinnaphte; Henri Sainte-Claire Deville got it from balsam of Tolu, the resin of a Colombian tree, in 1841 and saw its kinship to benzene; Berzelius proposed "toluin" in 1843 and Cahours settled "toluène" in 1850 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toluene); [Encyclopædia Universalis](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/henri-sainte-claire-deville/)). It is natural: 15–20 percent of coal-tar light oil and a minor constituent of petroleum ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/science/toluene)). The volumes in commerce are refinery product, made by catalytic reforming of naphtha, with smaller streams from steam-cracker pyrolysis gasoline, coal tar and styrene plants ([IARC](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/n/iarcmono047/a006b/); [ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp56-c5.pdf)).

*Uses, past and present.* Most toluene is never isolated; it stays in gasoline as an octane booster ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp56-c5.pdf); [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-09/documents/toluene.pdf)). Of the isolated share, nearly half is converted to benzene, a fifth to xylenes, 12 percent goes to solvents for paints, coatings, inks, adhesives and cleaners, and 8 percent to toluene diisocyanate for polyurethane foam ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp56-c5.pdf)). It is the feedstock for TNT, benzoic acid, saccharin, dyes and pharmaceuticals ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/science/toluene)), and it is in nail polish, fragrances and paint thinner at home ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-09/documents/toluene.pdf)). Gasoline production, transport and use is the largest emission source ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp56-c6.pdf)).

*Makers, then.* World production passed 5 million tonnes in 1980, over 85 percent of it in the United States, Western Europe and Japan; U.S. refiners drew 3.6 million tonnes of isolated toluene from reformate in 1978 ([IARC](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/n/iarcmono047/a006b/)). In this region the Toxics Release Inventory shows the older solvent economy: Nashua Tape in Watervliet reported 643 tonnes to air in 1988, SHPP (the former GE Plastics works) in Selkirk 470 tonnes in 1987, Universal Packaging in Saratoga Springs 421 tonnes in 1990, and the Fluorglas and Saint-Gobain fabric-coating plants in Hoosick Falls 54 tonnes in 1991. U.S. TRI releases nationally fell from about 26,000 tonnes in 2005 to 11,000 tonnes in 2015 ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp56-c6.pdf)).

*Makers, now.* Toluene is a commodity nobody stopped making. The global market was about 37 million tonnes in 2022, Asia-Pacific holding about 48 percent and China the leading producer; the largest named makers are Sinopec, PetroChina, CNOOC, Hengli, Lotte, GS Caltex, SK, Sinochem, Reliance and Bharat Petroleum ([ChemAnalyst via DRI](https://dri.co.jp/auto/report/chemanalyst/231001-global-toluene-market-analysis-plant.html)). ATSDR's list of 18 U.S. producers is refiners and petrochemical firms — ExxonMobil, Shell, Dow, BP, Citgo, Marathon, Valero, Flint Hills, Equistar, Total among them — and 2,198 U.S. facilities make, process or use it ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp56-c5.pdf)). Locally it is still the region's biggest industrial air release by mass: SHPP in Selkirk reported 27.2 tonnes to air in 2024 and MPM Silicones in Waterford 8.0 tonnes. No local water supply has a toluene result on record.

### How it gets here
*Air, mostly.* Toluene is the region's largest industrial air release by mass. SHPP in Selkirk, 15 miles from Westerlo on the Hudson, reported 27,216 kg to the air in 2024 and 469,532 kg in 1987. MPM Silicones in Waterford reported 7,983 kg in 2024 and 149,817 in 1987. Nashua Tape in Watervliet peaked at 643,147 kg in 1988 and stopped filing after 1996. Saint-Gobain at Hoosick Falls reported 3,418 kg in 2024; Curia in Rensselaer 647 kg, down from 40,389 in 1990; Norlite 129 kg; the Hudson fuel terminals a few hundred each. In air, toluene "rapidly breaks down by reacting with other chemicals or oxygen" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=160&toxid=29)), so the plateau, 15 to 30 miles upwind and uphill, receives little of it.

*Water, by permit.* Haleon's Oak Hill plant discharges to Catskill Creek 14.1 km down the Ten Mile chain; its outfall 002 ran a median of 1 µg/L against a 5 µg/L limit from FY2009 to FY2012, about 0.02 kg a year. CSX's Selkirk yard reports a median of 5 µg/L against a limit of 100 to the Hudson, FY2026. SHPP's three outfalls add roughly 3.5 kg a year to the Hudson.

*Spills, at home.* Toluene reaches water and soil "via accidental spills and leaking storage tanks" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=160&toxid=29)): a heating-oil tank, a gas can, a driveway. It then "will readily evaporate into the air or be degraded by bacteria" — days in air, weeks to months in groundwater. A bedrock well beside a leaking tank is the local scenario, not a stack 15 miles away.

*Not measured.* No toluene result of any kind — tap, well, creek or reservoir — exists in the record.

### What it does
*People.* Toluene goes to the nervous system through the lungs. "Breathing toluene can cause headaches, dizziness, and nausea"; low to moderate exposure brings "tiredness, confusion, weakness, drunken-type actions, memory loss"; long workplace exposure may cost hearing and colour vision, and repeated inhalation can "permanently damage the brain." "Exposure to high levels of toluene during pregnancy, such as those associated with solvent abuse, may lead to developmental effects." EPA finds "inadequate information to assess the carcinogenicity"; IARC calls it "not classifiable" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=160&toxid=29)). It is smelled before it is tasted, and long before 1,000 µg/L.

*Plants and animals.* Toluene is acutely toxic to fish and invertebrates at the milligram-per-litre level — New York's 6,000 µg/L aquatic value — but it evaporates and degrades too quickly to persist in a stream or build up in tissue (general literature; no fetched source). A creek spill is a short event; an aquifer leak lasts months.

### What is not known
Nothing has been tested. No district report on file lists toluene, no creek or reservoir sample includes it, and no plateau private well has been checked. Haleon's Catskill Creek outfall has no toluene line after FY2012.

## XYLENE

### What it is
Xylene is three related chemicals — ortho, meta and para — sold as a mixture: a colourless, sweet-smelling, flammable solvent used in paints, coatings, glues and pesticides, and a component of gasoline ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=295&toxid=53)). ATSDR lists wildfires and oil and gas extraction among its sources; the workbook classes it as synthetic because what reaches us is refined. It is toluene's companion, with one more methyl group, and behaves the same way: EPA's MCL is 10 mg/L (10,000 µg/L), set against "nervous system damage" from "discharge from petroleum factories; discharge from chemical factories" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)); New York's groundwater and tap standard is 5 µg/L under the same "principal organic contaminant" rule as toluene ([NYSDEC TOGS 1.1.1](https://extapps.dec.ny.gov/docs/water_pdf/togs111.pdf)). The two numbers differ by a factor of two thousand.

### Who made it, and who still does
*Discovery.* Auguste Cahours isolated xylene from wood tar in 1850 and named it for the Greek for wood ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylene)). It is not wholly man-made: crude oil holds about 0.5–1 percent xylenes, and coal-tar distillate carries the same three isomers, roughly half of them m-xylene ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylene); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/science/xylene)). Coke-oven light oil, a steel-industry by-product, is still 3–6 percent mixed xylenes ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp71-c5.pdf)). But the volumes in commerce come from refineries: catalytic reforming of naphtha over a platinum catalyst gives a reformate that is 18–33 percent xylenes, and the pyrolysis gasoline from ethylene crackers is about 20 percent ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp71-c5.pdf)).

*Uses, past and present.* The old uses were solvent and dye feedstock: lacquer and rubber-cement thinner, aviation fuel, xylidines for dyes, and xylene musk for perfume ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/science/xylene)). Today half or more of all xylene is separated into p-xylene and oxidised to terephthalic acid, the acid half of PET bottles and polyester fibre; 10–15 percent becomes o-xylene for phthalic anhydride, which goes into plasticisers and paints; about 1 percent becomes m-xylene for isophthalic acid; and 10–25 percent is simply blended back into gasoline ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp71-c5.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylene)). The remainder is solvent in paints, coatings, inks and adhesives, and in histology and dental labs ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylene)). The 0.94 µg/L of m-xylene in Cairo's tap in 2021 is the fuel-and-solvent trace, not the polyester stream.

*Makers, then.* For a century after Cahours the source was coal tar and wood tar, the by-product streams of gas works, coke ovens and charcoal burning ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/science/xylene)). Refinery reforming displaced coal tar in the mid-twentieth century (general literature; no fetched source). Locally the TRI record shows who used it: Hannay Reels in Westerlo reported 6,113 kg to air in 1991, Keymark in Fonda peaked at 40,969 kg in 1987, and SI Group at Rotterdam Junction reported 174,384 kg in 1988, all from coatings and resin work rather than manufacture of the chemical itself.

*Makers, now.* U.S. output of mixed xylenes was 4.5–9 million tonnes a year in 2017–2019, of which p-xylene was 2.3–4.5 million tonnes; the 2019 reporters were Marathon at Texas City, CITGO, Phillips 66 at Belle Chasse and Shell at Norco and Deer Park ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp71-c5.pdf)). The world market is Asian. More than 57 million tonnes of p-xylene were made in 2023, over 90 percent of it in Asia; the Middle East, western Europe and North America together made under 5 million tonnes ([ResourceWise](https://www.resourcewise.com/chemicals-blog/paraxylene-price-points-forecasts-from-firm-foundation)). China's capacity reached about 47 million tonnes a year in 2024, more than tripling in six years, led by Hengli and Zhejiang Petrochemical complexes of over 2 million tonnes each ([Argus](https://www.argusmedia.com/en/news-and-insights/market-opinion-and-analysis-blog/an-industry-heavyweight-the-significance-and-scale-of-chinese-paraxylene-capacities); [ResourceWise](https://www.resourcewise.com/chemicals-blog/paraxylene-price-points-forecasts-from-firm-foundation)). Nearly all of it becomes polyester. The gasoline share is what reaches a well.

### How it gets here
*The Westerlo filing.* Hannay Reels in Westerlo, 5 miles away, reported 6,113 kg of xylene to the air in 1991 and filed for three years from 1989 — the only TRI release on this page from inside the Hilltowns. Coating solvent, in air, thirty-five years ago; "in air, xylenes break down within a few days" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=295&toxid=53)). A plant that vented six tonnes a year also stored and handled it, and no groundwater sample from the site is on record.

*The big stack.* Holcim's Ravena cement plant, 17 miles east, reported 13,747 kg of xylene to the air in 2023, in its second year of filing for it. Keymark in Fonda, Saint-Gobain at Hoosick Falls and SI Group at Rotterdam Junction reported 1,415, 2,533 and 879 kg in 2024. SHPP at Selkirk sent 113 kg to the Hudson in 2001, the largest water release on the list; CSX's Selkirk yard reports a median of 5 µg/L against a limit of 65, max 10, in FY2026.

*Fuel, at the tap.* Cairo Water District's finished water (tap) read 0.94 µg/L of xylene on 14 April 2021, reported as m-xylene and labelled a fuel-related VOC, against New York's 5 µg/L. That is the only local detection of any solvent in a public supply. Xylenes "evaporate quickly from water and soil but can travel through soil into groundwater" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=295&toxid=53)); Cairo's valley-fill wells are reducing water — iron 879 µg/L, manganese 744, methane — where solvents degrade slowly.

### What it does
*People.* The nervous system, through the lungs. High levels cause "nose and throat discomfort and make breathing difficult," with "headaches, dizziness, and impaired reaction time"; long-term low exposure produces the same, and "exposure to xylenes can cause feelings of intoxication and irritation." EPA finds the data "inadequate to assess if xylenes cause cancer"; IARC calls it "not classifiable" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=295&toxid=53)). Cairo's 0.94 µg/L is a ten-thousandth of the federal MCL and a fifth of the state's; the state number is a solvent-in-groundwater policy, not a dose.

*Plants and animals.* Like toluene, xylene is toxic to fish and aquatic invertebrates at milligram-per-litre concentrations and does not persist or bioaccumulate; it evaporates from a stream in hours to days and is degraded by soil bacteria in weeks to months (general literature; no fetched source). Wildfire smoke is a natural source the plateau's forests can produce on their own.

### What is not known
Whether Cairo's m-xylene was resampled after April 2021, and where it comes from — a leaking tank near a well, or a fuel spill on the recharge area. Whether groundwater at Hannay Reels in Westerlo was ever sampled for solvents. No plateau private well and no creek, lake or reservoir has a xylene result, and no district other than Cairo has a xylene line in any report on file.
## TTHM

### What it is
Total trihalomethanes are four small molecules — chloroform, bromodichloromethane, dibromochloromethane and bromoform — that form when chlorine meets the natural organic carbon in water. They do not occur in nature. The treatment plant makes them, and the pipes keep making them. EPA's rule says it plainly: "disinfectants can react with naturally-occurring materials in the water to form byproducts," which "if consumed in excess of EPA's standard over many years, may increase health risks" ([EPA Stage 1 and 2 DBPR](https://www.epa.gov/dwreginfo/stage-1-and-stage-2-disinfectants-and-disinfection-byproducts-rules)). The limit is 80 µg/L (0.080 mg/L) ([EPA MCL table](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)), judged as a locational running annual average: the mean of the last four quarters at each sampling point, so one high quarter is not a violation. Chloroform, the largest of the four, "evaporates quickly into the air from water and soil" and "breaks down slowly, so it may travel long distances" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=52&toxid=16)).

### Who made it, and who still does
*Discovery.* Nobody makes trihalomethanes for tap water; they form in the plant. Johannes Rook in Rotterdam reported in 1974 that chlorinating water produced chloroform and three bromine-containing cousins, and Bellar and Lichtenberg at EPA's Cincinnati laboratory measured the same compounds in U.S. municipal water the same year ([DeMarini 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7640377)). Chlorine oxidises the natural organic matter reservoirs carry; bromide in the source shifts the mix toward the brominated forms ([DeMarini 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7640377); [Wikipedia, bromoform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bromoform)). EPA's first rule, in November 1979, set an annual average of 100 µg/L; Stage 1 in 1998 cut it to 80 µg/L, and Stage 2 in 2006 made each sampling point meet it as a running annual average ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/dwreginfo/stage-1-and-stage-2-disinfectants-and-disinfection-byproducts-rules); [DeMarini 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7640377)). Albany's 2025 average was 42.7 µg/L; one Rensselaerville quarter hit 84.2.

*Uses, past and present.* The molecules themselves are old commodities. Samuel Guthrie made chloroform in 1831 by distilling chloride of lime with alcohol in a copper still at Sackets Harbor, New York, in the same year as Soubeiran in France and Liebig in Germany; Dumas named it in 1834 ([U.S. Army, Fort Drum](https://www.army.mil/article/252758/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroform)). James Young Simpson used it in an Edinburgh delivery on November 8, 1847 ([RCPE](https://www.rcpe.ac.uk/heritage/james-young-simpson)); the last recorded Western anaesthetic use was 1987 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroform)). It was a dry-cleaning solvent, a fire-extinguisher fluid, a pesticide intermediate and a cough-medicine ingredient until FDA banned it from drugs, cosmetics and food packaging in 1976 ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp6-c5.pdf)). Bromoform, made by Löwig in 1832, served as a solvent, sedative and flame retardant and is now a laboratory density liquid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bromoform)). Today chloroform's job is feedstock for HCFC-22, the refrigerant and the precursor of PTFE and other fluoropolymers ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp6-c5.pdf)).

*Makers, then.* Chloroform is made by chlorinating methane or methyl chloride at 400–500 °C and distilling the chloromethanes apart ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroform)). In the United States Dow ran the largest chloromethane plants until October 5, 2015, when Olin took over Dow's chlor-alkali and global chlorinated-organics businesses ([Olin 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000074303/000095015715001136/ex99-1.htm)). R-22 as a refrigerant was barred from new equipment in 2010 and from production and import for servicing in 2020 ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ods-phaseout/phaseout-class-ii-ozone-depleting-substances)); chloroform demand held because HCFC-22 is still the route to fluoropolymers ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp6-c5.pdf)).

*Makers, now.* U.S. production was 113,000–227,000 tonnes a year in 2016–2019, reported by Olin at Freeport, Texas; Occidental at Wichita, Geismar and La Porte; Shintech at Plaquemine, Louisiana; and EMD in Massachusetts ([ATSDR](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp6-c5.pdf)). Chinese output is larger and rising: chloroform emissions from eastern China grew by about 49,000 tonnes a year between 2010 and 2015, the whole global increase, tracking a fluoropolymer capacity headed for 230,000 tonnes by 2020 ([Fang et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-018-0278-2)). None of it is bought by a water district. The chloroform in Rensselaerville's pipes was made in Rensselaerville, from its own chlorine and leaf litter.

### How it gets here
*Made in the plant.* Every surface-water supply in the study area chlorinates, and every one draws raw water carrying 3–6 mg/L of dissolved organic carbon off forest, pasture and wetland. Carbon plus chlorine is the whole recipe. Bedrock wells carry far less carbon, which is why the groundwater districts read low: Greenville 13.9 µg/L (11 Sept 2025), Cairo 19.44 µg/L (14 Sept 2022), both single samples. The reservoir systems run three to four times higher.

*Made in the pipes.* Formation continues as long as free chlorine and carbon share the water, so the number climbs with water age and warmth, and it is highest in late summer. Rensselaerville's 2025 annual average was 43.7 µg/L, but one quarter reached 84.2 — above the 80 line, not a violation, because the rule averages. Albany's running average (Alcove and Basic Creek through the Feura Bush plant) was 50.2 in 2019, 48.7 in 2020, 55.07 in 2021, 58.5 in 2023 and 42.7 in 2025. Catskill's running average stood at 62.1 µg/L on 24 Feb 2022, and the workbook records a violation there in 2022.

*Not from wells.* A private bedrock well is not chlorinated, so it makes none unless the owner shock-chlorinates it. Every number above is finished water (tap) from a public system.

### What it does
*People.* EPA lists "Liver, kidney or central nervous system problems; increased risk of cancer" ([EPA MCL table](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)). For chloroform, long-term exposure "can damage the liver and kidneys"; the Department of Health and Human Services calls it "reasonably anticipated to be a human carcinogen," and EPA calls it "likely to be carcinogenic to humans by all routes of exposure at high dose levels" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=52&toxid=16)). The scale is decades above the limit modestly raising bladder-cancer risk. Because chloroform leaves water so readily, a hot shower is also an inhalation route (general literature; no fetched source).

*Plants and animals.* Laboratory animals "that drank chloroform for a long time developed cancer of the liver and kidneys," and those that breathed it suffered nasal damage that "worsened with longer exposure periods" ([ATSDR](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=52&toxid=16)). In the open they are a tap-water problem, not a creek problem: they evaporate and disperse, and no surface-water or well reading exists in the record. Chlorinated sewage effluent carries some into streams, but no local permit measures it (general literature; no fetched source).

### What is not known
Which quarter and which tap gave Rensselaerville its 84.2 µg/L, and whether 2026 repeats it. Greenville and Cairo have one sample each, not running averages, and no Cairo value after September 2022 is in the record. Catskill's 2022 violation has no later running average behind it. No well that is shock-chlorinated has ever been tested afterward.

## HAA5

### What it is
Haloacetic acids are acetic acid — vinegar's acid — with one, two or three of its hydrogens swapped for chlorine or bromine. The regulated five are monochloroacetic, dichloroacetic, trichloroacetic, monobromoacetic and dibromoacetic acid ([Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/publications/healthy-living/guidelines-canadian-drinking-water-quality-guideline-technical-document-haloacetic-acids.html)). They do not occur in nature; like the trihalomethanes they form when chlorine reacts with the natural organic carbon in raw water, in the plant and on through the mains. The limit is 60 µg/L (0.060 mg/L) as a locational running annual average ([EPA MCL table](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)). Unlike the trihalomethanes they do not evaporate; they are barely volatile and "can be broken down by microorganisms within distribution networks" ([Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/publications/healthy-living/guidelines-canadian-drinking-water-quality-guideline-technical-document-haloacetic-acids.html)), so a long, slow main can lose haloacetic acids while it keeps gaining trihalomethanes.

### Who made it, and who still does
*Discovery.* Haloacetic acids were found in chlorinated drinking water in 1983, nine years after the trihalomethanes, as the chlorination products of humic substances ([IARC vol. 84](https://publications.iarc.who.int/_publications/media/download/2679/8fb46d348ed219ee943bf9bfe48f166ab592b5fa.pdf)). The regulated five are mono-, di- and trichloroacetic acid and mono- and dibromoacetic acid ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haloacetic_acids)); chloral hydrate forms beside them ([Wikipedia, chloral hydrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloral_hydrate)). EPA's Stage 1 rule of 1998 set 60 µg/L, and Stage 2 in 2006 applied it per location as a running annual average ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/dwreginfo/stage-1-and-stage-2-disinfectants-and-disinfection-byproducts-rules)). Nobody makes them for water; the plant does. Albany's 2025 average was 20.6 µg/L, Rensselaerville's 31.2.

*Uses, past and present.* Each acid has an industrial life of its own. Félix LeBlanc made impure chloroacetic acid in 1843 by chlorinating acetic acid in sunlight, and Hoffmann and Wurtz made it pure in 1857 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroacetic_acid)). Dumas made trichloroacetic acid in 1839, a founding case for the theory of organic radicals ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichloroacetic_acid)). Liebig made chloral in 1832, and Oscar Liebreich introduced chloral hydrate as a sleeping draught in 1869 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloral_hydrate)). Monochloroacetic acid is the big one: about 420,000 tonnes a year go into carboxymethyl cellulose for food and drilling mud, glyphosate, the phenoxy herbicides 2,4-D and MCPA, and thioglycolic acid for PVC stabilisers and hair perms ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroacetic_acid)). Dichloroacetic acid, made by hydrolysing dichloroacetyl chloride from trichloroethylene, is an intermediate for glyoxylic acid and sulfonamides, an analytical reagent in polyester fibre work, and a medical cautery ([IARC vol. 84](https://publications.iarc.who.int/_publications/media/download/2679/8fb46d348ed219ee943bf9bfe48f166ab592b5fa.pdf)). Trichloroacetic acid precipitates proteins in the lab, peels skin and burns off warts in the clinic; its sodium salt was sold as a herbicide in the 1950s and is now tightly regulated ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichloroacetic_acid)).

*Makers, then.* Chloroacetic acids come from the same chlorine-and-acetic-acid chemistry the dye and pharmaceutical firms of the nineteenth century built, and the making of one leaves the others as impurities ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroacetic_acid)). In 2002 IARC counted two dichloroacetic acid producers in the United States and one each in China, Japan and Mexico ([IARC vol. 84](https://publications.iarc.who.int/_publications/media/download/2679/8fb46d348ed219ee943bf9bfe48f166ab592b5fa.pdf)). The herbicide market for sodium trichloroacetate fell away under regulation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichloroacetic_acid)).

*Makers, now.* Nouryon, the former AkzoNobel specialty chemicals business, calls itself one of the world's largest monochloroacetic acid makers, with plants at Delfzijl in the Netherlands, Taixing in China and LeMoyne, Alabama ([Nouryon](https://www.nouryon.com/products/monochloroacetic-acid/)). Chinese producers supply most of the rest of the 420,000 tonnes (general literature; no fetched source). Di- and trichloroacetic acid remain small specialty products. What a Hilltowns tap carries is none of that: it is the sum of what chlorine did to Basic Creek's dissolved carbon in the hours between the filter plant and the kitchen, and it decays in days to weeks in the pipe.

### How it gets here
*Same recipe, same plants.* Raw dissolved organic carbon of 3–6 mg/L meets chlorine at every surface-water plant in the area, and the acids follow the trihalomethanes down the pipe. The pattern by district matches: the groundwater systems are lowest — Greenville 2.2 µg/L (11 Sept 2025), Cairo 11.8 µg/L (14 Sept 2022), each a single sample — and the reservoir systems higher.

*The reservoir systems.* Albany's running average (Alcove and Basic Creek through Feura Bush) was 17.4 µg/L in 2019, 17.9 in 2020, 19.7 in 2021, 18.4 in 2023 and 20.6 in 2025, a flat line at a third of the limit. Rensselaerville averaged 31.2 µg/L in 2025. Catskill's running average was 39.84 µg/L on 24 Feb 2022, the highest in the record and two-thirds of the limit; Catskill is the one system that violated the trihalomethane limit that year, and its acids tracked.

*Not from wells, not from creeks.* Every one of the nine results is finished water (tap) from a public system. A private bedrock well makes none unless chlorine is added. No raw creek, lake or well result exists, and no discharge permit within the study area measures the acids.

### What it does
*People.* EPA's table gives one line: "Increased risk of cancer" ([EPA MCL table](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)). California's health agency sets its no-significant-risk goals for dichloroacetic acid (0.2 µg/L), trichloroacetic acid (0.1 µg/L) and dibromoacetic acid (0.03 µg/L) on carcinogenicity, "at a level of risk of one additional cancer case per one million persons exposed over a lifetime" ([OEHHA](https://oehha.ca.gov/water/crnr/haloacetic-acids-drinking-water)). Those goals sit two to three orders of magnitude below the federal limit and below every local result; the difference is the gap between a one-in-a-million target and an enforceable standard. Health Canada also reviews reproductive and developmental toxicity for each of the five ([Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/publications/healthy-living/guidelines-canadian-drinking-water-quality-guideline-technical-document-haloacetic-acids.html)). The route is drinking; there is no shower route, because the acids stay in the water.

*Plants and animals.* Dichloroacetic acid "caused liver tumors in laboratory animals" ([Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/publications/healthy-living/guidelines-canadian-drinking-water-quality-guideline-technical-document-haloacetic-acids.html)). Outside a chlorinated main, exposure is small: the acids biodegrade in days to weeks, and no stream, lake or soil measurement exists here. Chlorinated sewage effluent carries some to receiving creeks (general literature; no fetched source).

### What is not known
Greenville and Cairo are single samples, not running averages, and Cairo has no value after September 2022. Rensselaerville's 2025 average hides its quarterly spread; the trihalomethane spike to 84.2 that year has no matching acid figure in the record. No plateau private well, chlorinated or not, has been tested.

## TP

### What it is
Phosphorus is a nutrient, the element every cell needs, and in a lake the one in shortest supply. Add it and algae grow; add enough and cyanobacteria take over. Natural in New York soils, it binds to sediment for decades and comes back out of oxygen-starved mud. It is not toxic; the harm arrives as blooms and anoxia. EPA's drinking-water table has no phosphorus line ([EPA MCL table](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)). The numbers that govern it are for lakes: New York's guidance of 20 µg/L, written into the Basic Creek Reservoir TMDL as "the epilimnetic summer mean total phosphorus level shall not exceed 20 µg/L" ([NYSDEC TMDL](https://extapps.dec.ny.gov/docs/water_pdf/tmdlbasicck.pdf)); 15 µg/L for New York City's reservoirs; nothing numeric for streams. Too much of it "cause[s] excessive algal growth which can harm aquatic life and, in many instances, produce toxins" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/nutrientpollution)).

### How it gets here
*Off the farms, mostly.* The 2013 TMDL puts the external load to Basic Creek Reservoir at 2,082 lb a year, about 944 kg. Agriculture supplies 1,310 lb (594 kg) in runoff and 409 lb (186 kg) below ground; natural groundwater 185 lb (84 kg); forest under 80 lb (36 kg); developed land, septic included, about 92 lb (42 kg) ([NYSDEC TMDL](https://extapps.dec.ny.gov/docs/water_pdf/tmdlbasicck.pdf)). About a third arrives underground, through till and shale, where no buffer strip intercepts it. Residence time is 0.1 year — five weeks — so the reservoir holds roughly what the land sends. Summer means were 42 µg/L in 2004 and 55 in 2005 against the 20 target; the TMDL requires a 48% cut. EPA's general list matches: manure and fertilizer, stormwater off "rooftops, sidewalks and roads," septic systems, "soaps and detergents, as well as yard fertilizers and pet waste" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/nutrientpollution/sources-and-solutions)).

*Off the bottom.* The TMDL set the internal load at zero. But Basic Creek's bottom water went to 0.04 mg/L of oxygen in July 2013 and 0.91 in August 2018, and low oxygen "may increase the release of precipitated phosphates, encouraging the proliferation of nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria" ([EPA CADDIS](https://www.epa.gov/caddis/dissolved-oxygen)). Thompsons Lake showed the signature on 17 Sept 2025: 0.19343 mg/L at 14.6 m depth.

*Elsewhere in the record.* Vly Creek Reservoir read 0.337 mg/L in Aug and Sept 2016. Across 457 lake results (1989–2025, eleven lakes) the median is 0.0204 mg/L, on the 20 µg/L line, with 14 non-detects; streams run lower, median 0.0178 mg/L. No permitted outfall in the Basic Creek watershed reports phosphorus; the Greenville plant's permit has no line for it. The permits that measure it sit in other valleys.

### What it does
*People.* Phosphorus itself does nothing to a person at these levels. Its effects are downstream: bloom toxins that bring "Rashes, Stomach or liver illness, Respiratory problems, Neurological effects," and byproducts formed when disinfectants react with bloom-laden water, linked to "reproductive and developmental problems" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/nutrientpollution/effects-human-health)). Basic Creek's algal carbon becomes part of Albany's trihalomethanes.

*Plants and animals.* Blooms "block sunlight from underwater plants," their decay pulls oxygen down "to a level that can no longer support aquatic life," and even non-toxic blooms harm fish by "clogging their gills" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/nutrientpollution/effects-environment)). A bloom eats the phosphorus, dies, and in dying strips the oxygen that frees more phosphorus from the mud — the loop a shallow reservoir runs every warm, still week.

### What is not known
Whether the 48% cut has happened: the 2004 and 2005 summer means are the last Basic Creek results expressed as the TMDL measures them. The internal load, set at zero in 2013, has never been measured. No phosphorus result exists for any septic drainfield or private well on the plateau.

## FECAL

### What it is
Fecal coliform and *E. coli* are gut bacteria of warm-blooded animals — people, cattle, deer, geese — and in water they are a marker, not the disease. "Most strains of enterococci and *E. coli* do not cause human illness (that is, they are not human pathogens); rather, they indicate the presence of fecal contamination" ([EPA 2012 RWQC](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-10/documents/rwqc2012.pdf)). EPA's drinking-water table: "fecal coliforms and *E. coli* only come from human and animal fecal waste" ([EPA MCL table](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations)). The numbers: New York closes a beach at 235 *E. coli* per 100 mL in a single sample; a Class A stream is held to 200 fecal coliform; sewage plants to 200 monthly and 400 weekly as geometric means. EPA's 2012 swimming criteria ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/wqc/2012-recreational-water-quality-criteria)) are an *E. coli* geometric mean of 126 per 100 mL with no more than 10% of samples over 410, at 36 illnesses per 1,000 swimmers ([EPA 2012 RWQC](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-10/documents/rwqc2012.pdf)). Bacteria persist days to weeks in water, longer in sediment.

### How it gets here
*Rain over pasture.* Every one of the 80 stream results (2001–2008) is a detection, and the median is 280 per 100 mL, above the 200 Class A line. The highs: Coeymans Creek 4,000 (4 Aug and 16 Oct 2003), Onesquethaw Creek 4,000 (28 Oct 2008), Basic Creek 2,000 (28 Oct 2008), Fox Creek 1,840 (25 Sept 2001). Manure on fields and cattle in the stream are the plateau's obvious source; wildlife adds a baseline. These are raw creek readings.

*Septic and sewage.* Two permitted plants discharge to Basic Creek tributaries above the reservoir. Camp Malka's outfall reported a median of 10.1 per 100 mL in FY2026 (maximum 387.3, limit 400) after a first-year median of 189.95 in FY2024; the Greenville plant reported a median of 10. Berne's plant, on Fox Creek, reported a median of 1 in FY2026 but a maximum of 2,420 against its 400 limit. The plateau's septic systems report nothing.

*Into a well.* NYSDOH names the routes: "a missing or defective well cap," "contaminant seepage through the well casing," seepage "along the outside of the well casing," and "well flooding" ([NYSDOH](https://www.health.ny.gov/environmental/water/drinking/coliform_bacteria.htm)). A positive *E. coli* result "should be considered indication of fecal pollution."

### What it does
*People.* Swallowing contaminated water brings gastrointestinal illness; EPA's criteria are set on "diarrhea (three or more loose stools in a 24 hour period), vomiting, nausea and stomachache" within ten to twelve days of swimming ([EPA 2012 RWQC](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-10/documents/rwqc2012.pdf)). Of the pathogens the indicator stands for, "Six types of *E. coli* can cause diarrheal illness in people," and Shiga toxin-producing strains "can lead to a serious health condition called hemolytic uremic syndrome"; children under 5, adults 65 and older and people with weakened immune systems are most at risk ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/ecoli/about/index.html)). NYSDOH advises testing a well "at least once a year," in late spring or early summer, when "contamination is most likely to show up" ([NYSDOH](https://www.health.ny.gov/environmental/water/drinking/coliform_bacteria.htm)).

*Plants and animals.* The bacteria come from animals and mostly go back to them: livestock drinking from a manured stream recycle their own pathogens, and Shiga-toxin strains live harmlessly in cattle guts (general literature; no fetched source). The garden watered from a creek in August is the local crop route. In sediment the bacteria outlast their time in the water.

### What is not known
No stream count exists after 28 Oct 2008. No raw-water count from Basic Creek Reservoir or Alcove is in the record, and no lake or beach count at all. No private well on the plateau appears. The permit results mix CFU, MPN and unlabeled counts, so plant-to-plant comparison is approximate.

## DO

### What it is
Dissolved oxygen is the contaminant that harms by its absence. It is what fish and insects breathe. It is a state, not a substance: it enters "mainly from the atmosphere," from groundwater, and from plants by day; "cold water can hold more dissolved oxygen than warm water," and summer surface water "is typically more than 8 milligrams per liter" ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/dissolved-oxygen-and-water)). Below 2 mg/L "the water is defined as hypoxic," and "the hypoxia kills many organisms that cannot escape" ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/dissolved-oxygen-and-water)). New York's standard for non-trout waters: "the minimum daily average shall not be less than 5.0 mg/L, and at no time shall the DO concentration be less than 4.0 mg/L"; for trout waters 6.0 and 5.0; for trout spawning "not less than 7.0 mg/L" ([6 NYCRR 703.3](https://regulations.justia.com/states/new-york/title-6/chapter-x/subchapter-a/article-2/part-703/section-703-3)). Sewage permits set a floor, usually 7 mg/L in effluent.

### How it gets here
*Warmth and rot.* "Organic decomposition consumes oxygen" ([EPA CADDIS](https://www.epa.gov/caddis/dissolved-oxygen)); "excess organic material in lakes and rivers can cause eutrophic conditions, which is an oxygen-deficient situation" ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/dissolved-oxygen-and-water)). Basic Creek Reservoir is 3.5–4 m deep and does not stratify, yet its bottom water fell to 0.04 mg/L in July 2013 and 0.91 in August 2018, below every line in the standard, while iron and manganese rose. Bloom carbon is the fuel.

*Day and night.* Nutrients "stimulate oxygen-generating (photosynthesis) and oxygen-depleting (respiration) processes," so "DO supersaturation may occur during the day, with decreased DO at night" ([EPA CADDIS](https://www.epa.gov/caddis/dissolved-oxygen)). The record's highest readings are that daytime swing: Fox Creek 151.5% of saturation (20 Aug 2015), Catskill Creek 146% (11 Sept 2012), Basic Creek 117% (11 Sept 2012), Lawson Lake 122% (21 July 2015). A stream at 150% in the afternoon is full of algae; its night reading is not in the record. Across 147 stream results (1984–2025) the median is 9.4 mg/L; across 655 lake results (2013–2023) it is 8.43. All are raw readings.

*Effluent.* The permit number is a floor, so the "over limit" flags in the discharge table mean the opposite of what they mean elsewhere on this page: Berne's plant at 8.95 mg/L against a 5 floor is complying. The Cass Residential Center, 3.3 km off on a Fox Creek tributary, reported 3.6 mg/L against its 7 floor in FY2009 and 8.53 by FY2019. Norlite's outfall 006 (watchlist, 44.9 km) reported 0 mg/L in FY2019 and FY2020.

### What it does
*People.* The human effects are secondhand. Bottom water without oxygen releases iron, manganese and phosphorus from the mud; the first two are what a reservoir intake draws in August, and the third feeds the next bloom, whose carbon becomes trihalomethanes in the plant. Cairo's valley-fill wells, reducing at 879 µg/L iron and 744 µg/L manganese, are the groundwater version.

*Plants and animals.* The order of loss is fixed: "Large fish of a given species die first, whereas small fish may be alive," and "Species with the highest oxygen requirements die"; mayflies, stoneflies and caddisflies give way to "tolerant worms and fly larvae" ([EPA CADDIS](https://www.epa.gov/caddis/dissolved-oxygen)). A trout stream needs 6 mg/L as a daily average. Low oxygen at the sediment "may increase the release of precipitated phosphates, encouraging the proliferation of nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria" ([EPA CADDIS](https://www.epa.gov/caddis/dissolved-oxygen)) — the bloom loop closes.

### What is not known
Whether Basic Creek's bottom goes to near-zero every summer or only in 2013 and 2018; no overnight record exists for any lake or stream here, and the night minimum is what the standard is written for. Alcove, Lake Myosotis and Thompsons Lake have no oxygen profile in the record. No lake reading is dated after 2023.
